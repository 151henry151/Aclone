# Development backlog

**World-builder configurability applies throughout this backlog.** Meaningful
gameplay switches, limits, costs, rates, capacities, permissions and policies must
use world settings or existing editable catalogues, with stated defaults,
validation, editor help, persistence and portable world designs. Reuse one source
of truth across simulation, UI and NPCs. Test changed values as well as defaults,
and preserve existing saved state when policies change. Client graphics quality
and technical performance controls remain in their appropriate layer.

## Planned — Taking off from the runway

Full throttle for the length of the runway, then pulling up, leaves the airplane on the ground.

**What happens now.** The biplane is mode 2. `fly` in `src/shared/flight.ts` stays grounded until all of these are true at once: ground speed at least `FLIGHT.rotationSpeed` (19 m/s, about 42 mph), nose above about 4° (`pitch > 0.07`), upward acceleration above 0.4 m/s², and the wheel brake not held. On the ground the nose is clamped to roughly −2° through +17°, and with the stick released it settles toward level as speed rises. Pull-up authority on the ground is only `pitchInput * 0.55 * authority`, and authority itself scales with speed over 22 m/s. Puddlewick's marked strip is 240 m (220 m on a smaller parish). "Make a landing possible" records that takeoff already works; a full-length full-throttle run that never leaves the pavement shows that it does not.

**What to change.** Find why that run never meets the lift-off gate, and make a takeoff something a pilot can do: full throttle along the public runway, then pull up, and the wheels leave the ground before the pavement ends. A moderate load may lengthen the run; it must not make the public strip impossible. Keep the crash rules for a wing, a building, the water and a dive. Do not weaken the landing work in "Make a landing possible" in order to get airborne, and do not add an automatic rotate. The stick pull is what raises the nose.

**Per-world.** Rotation speed, the pitch and lift needed to unstick, and the public runway length stay world rules. The default, including on Puddlewick, is a takeoff that fits the public strip.

**Tests.** On the real graded runway, full throttle from one threshold and a pull-up once the tail is flying leaves the ground before the far threshold, and the same run with the stick neutral stays down. A short strip that is genuinely too short still stays down. Water, a building and a steep bank still destroy the airframe.

## Planned — A real highway tractor, and a gradual step off the pavement

The on-road tractor-trailer is too simple, and leaving the pavement throws it from road speed down to a walk.

**What happens now.** `createHighwayTractor` in `src/client/highway-model.ts` builds the unit from boxes: two frame rails, a cab of separate panels, a fifth-wheel disc, and three unmarked cream discs whose needles track speed, fuel and condition. Parts do not read as one truck. Frame, axles, tanks, steps, bumper and cab have gaps, so pieces look disconnected or hung in the air. The cab has a wheel and three needles, not a gauge cluster a driver can read. `highwaySurface` calls the rig a field the instant any body sample is off both pavement and dirt road, and returns `offRoadSpeed` from `data/automotive.json`, **1.4 m/s (about 3 mph)**. `move` then sets `p.speed` to that cap in one step (`src/shared/simulation.ts`). A rig doing about 40 mph becomes a 3 mph crawl in a single tick. The paved top is about 65 mph and a dirt road stays about 45 mph; only the open field is the 3 mph cliff. Semitrailers are the same family of simple geometry in `createSemitrailer`.

**What to change.**

1. **The truck.** Look at photographs of a conventional tractor unit and at a render of this model (side, front, rear, underside and the driver's seat). Rebuild it so the frame, axles, springs, tanks, steps, bumper, mudguards, cab and fifth wheel actually meet. Nothing that should be bolted to the frame may float. Fill in the missing frame and running gear rather than adding ornaments on top of the gaps. Do the same for the semitrailer that couples to it: landing gear, frame, floor and rear doors have to connect.
2. **The cluster.** Replace the three discs with a readable cluster in the cab: a speedometer in mph, a fuel gauge, and the condition instrument the needles already track, each with a scale and a needle driven by the live value. The same numbers must be right from the driver's seat and wrong from nowhere else. Follow the biplane's gauge approach in `src/client/biplane.ts` where it already solves a readable face.
3. **Leaving the road.** Stepping off the pavement slows the rig with its brakes, toward a field pace of about **12 mph (about 5.4 m/s)**, instead of snapping from road speed to 3 mph. Regaining the pavement uses ordinary acceleration back up, not a snap either. A dirt road keeps its present dirt-road pace. The whole rig, tractor and semitrailer, shares one answer.

**Per-world.** Field pace stays `driving.offRoadSpeed`, default about 12 mph, including on Puddlewick. Dirt-road pace stays `dirtSpeed`. Braking rate stays the highway braking rule. A world can still choose a slower field.

**Tests.** A rig at about 40 mph that puts a wheel onto the field is still well above 3 mph on the next tick, and it reaches about 12 mph by braking rather than in one step. Sitting fully off the pavement, the cap is about 12 mph. A dirt road is still about 45 mph, and pavement is still about 65 mph. A render of the tractor and a coupled semitrailer shows the frame, axles, tanks, steps and fifth wheel meeting, and the seated view shows three marked gauges whose needles match speed, fuel and condition.

## Planned — Hovercraft turns should drift

A hovercraft currently corners like a wheeled tractor. Turning the nose should not instantly turn the direction of travel.

**What happens now.** The Marsh Courier is mode 4. In `move`, modes 0, 1 and 4 share one steering step: heading changes by `v.turn`, and the next position is `sin(heading) * speed` along that new heading. There is no sideways velocity. A full lock at speed pivots the path with the nose. The model, skirt and fan in `src/client/hovercraft.ts` are unchanged by this item.

**What to change.** Keep a velocity that does not have to point along the nose. Yaw still follows the stick, with less authority at low speed so it can spin up and dock. The old direction of travel persists and bleeds off, so a turn at speed slides outward and the nose leads the path. Stopping, or holding the new heading, lets the drift die and the craft run true again. Reverse stays a slow controlled shove, not a slide across the map. Boats, tractors and the truck do not gain this drift.

**Per-world.** A hovercraft drift setting, default on for the Marsh Courier, including on Puddlewick. It scales how long the old direction hangs on. Zero restores today's pivot.

**Tests.** At speed, a sustained turn leaves part of the velocity along the old heading for a noticeable interval, and the path is wider than the same turn with drift off. Coming out of the turn, the velocity settles back along the nose. A tractor given the same stick does not drift. Drift off matches the current pivot.

## Planned — Seaplanes

An amphibian: one aircraft that lands on the runway and on open water. The pilot has two passenger seats, and the hold is large for an aircraft without becoming a freighter.

**What happens now.** The only aircraft is the biplane (slot 2, mode 2, a 90-weight hold). In `fly`, any contact with the water is `'water impact'` and the airframe is destroyed, before the landing checks run (`support < seaLevel - 0.12` and again once the wheels are down). A person who walks into deep water is sent back to the parish spawn. Passengers already ride by `hitch` on boats; aircraft have no seats. The biplane is bought and boarded at the airfield hangar.

**The aircraft.** A new slot, an amphibian with a hull or floats and wheels that raise and lower. High wing, a cockpit for the pilot, and a cabin with two seats and a window each. It uses `fly` with an amphibious flag, the same crash notice, and the pilot licence where licences are on. It is bought and boarded at the hangar, like the biplane. A starting price sits above the biplane's 135s, around **220s**, checked in the balance test.

**Two passengers and the pilot.** The cabin takes the pilot and **two** passengers. A third is refused. They board only while the aircraft is stopped, on the apron or moored beside a shore or boathouse, within the ordinary nearby distance. Nobody boards in flight, and a passenger cannot fly it. Their own bags stay the small per-seat allowance from the passenger-flight entry (on the order of 20–30 weight). That allowance is separate from the hold.

**The hold.** About **400 weight**, the size of the parish box trailer. That is about four times the biplane's 90, and under the quarry tipping trailer's 1,000 and the planned cargo aircraft at 1,000–1,500. Heavy goods fill it the same way they fill a trailer: fuel and grain pack a long way, stone and ore fill it sooner. Loading and unloading happen only while stopped on the apron or moored, through the same carry flow the biplane uses until the shared aircraft hold exists. A load past the cap is refused. Weight aboard lengthens the takeoff run, on the runway and on the water, and raises the speed the hull needs before it will fly.

**Landing and taking off.**

- On land the wheels are down. It uses the runway and the same contact rules as the biplane, including whatever "Make a landing possible" relaxes them to. Wheels up on the pavement is a crash.
- On water the wheels are up. The water surface is the runway: the same sink, pitch and bank checks, measured against the water, and a surviving touchdown leaves it afloat. Wheels down on the water is a crash. The biplane still breaks up on any water contact.
- The water has to be deep enough that the hull clears the bottom (about a metre) and long enough for the run (on the order of 200–300 m of clear water). Open sea and wide inland water qualify. A narrow river, a short pond, or a sea with waves above a modest height does not: the landing is refused, or it is a crash if the pilot forces it. Once afloat it taxis with the throttle and rudder, and B slows it. It moors at a shore or boathouse the way a boat does. It does not drive up the beach.
- Takeoff is a run, on the runway or on the step, then rotate. Hull drag means the water run is longer than the biplane's ground roll, and a full hold makes it longer again.

**A crash.** On land, follow the passenger-flight rule: nobody dies, everyone is set down on foot at the site, and cargo that will not fit a walking load stays there for a vehicle to collect. On water, put the pilot and passengers on foot at the nearest shore. Do not use the deep-water path that sends a walker back to the parish spawn. Cargo that will not fit them stays afloat at the site for a boat to collect. Fares, if this aircraft is later used for the paid flights, refund as that entry already says.

**Per-world.** Seat count (default two passengers), hold capacity (default about 400 weight), price, whether water landings are allowed, the minimum depth, run length and wave height, and whether the pilot licence applies. The default is this amphibian, including on Puddlewick. A world can turn water landings off and leave it a land plane with the same cabin and hold.

**Tests.** A biplane that meets the water is destroyed; a seaplane that meets the water inside the landing limits is afloat, and one outside them is destroyed. It lands on the runway with the wheels down and stops, and takes off from the runway and from calm water. Wheels down on the water, and wheels up on the pavement, are crashes. Two passengers can board while it is stopped; a third cannot, and nobody can board in flight. The hold accepts a load up to about 400 weight and refuses more, and that cap stays above the biplane's 90 and below a tipping trailer's 1,000. A water crash leaves the people on the nearest shore, and does not move them to the parish spawn.

## Planned — Walking speed follows snow depth

On foot, snow does not slow you down. A tractor in the same field does. Walking should use that same depth.

**What happens now.** "On foot" is slot 5, mode 8, with a speed of 5 m/s (about 11 mph), or about 19 mph while Shift is held. In `move`, a vehicle only consults the ground when its mode is 0, 1 or 4. Walking is mode 8, so it always receives a surface factor of 1. Dry ground, mud, 4 cm of snow and a metre of snow are the same pace. The playing guide says walking keeps its wet-surface handling and is not slowed by snow depth.

Tractors do read the snow. `drivingSurface` in `src/shared/driving-surface.ts` keeps a road at wet-road speed (about 90% of dry, because any snow on the ground raises road wetness to at least 0.7) and, off the road, uses `snowSpeed = 0.06 + 0.34 / (1 + 7·(depth − 4 cm))`. That is about **40%** of dry-road speed in the ordinary 4 cm layer, about **10%** in a metre, and it approaches a **6%** crawl in a deeper drift. The change blends across a 2 m verge. Boost and reverse are included. Aircraft are not.

**What to change.** While walking, multiply that speed cap — the ordinary pace and the Shift pace — by the same `drivingSurface` speed factor a tractor gets at that spot. On a road, walking stays near full pace. In open snow it drops along the tractor curve: a brisk walk in 4 cm, a trudge in a deep drift. Use the same 2 m blend at the verge, so stepping off the lane slows you gradually. Turning can take the same grip reduction tractors take; do not invent a separate snow formula for feet. People and residents share `move`, so a resident on foot slows in the same place. Boats, aircraft, tractors and the ostrich stay as they are.

When roads later keep their own snow (the ploughing entry), walking reads that depth through the same lookup. An unploughed lane slows a person the way it slows a tractor; a ploughed lane does not. Until that exists, roads remain the clear, wet surface they are today.

**Per-world.** A world rule turns this on or off, and may scale how hard field snow pulls the walk down. The default is on, using the tractor curve with no extra scale, including on Puddlewick. The curve itself stays the one tractors use.

**Tests.** Off a road, 4 cm of snow cuts the walk cap to about 40% of dry, and a metre cuts it to about 10%, both with and without Shift. On a road in that same snow the cap stays at the wet-road pace, and the verge between them is gradual. Bare ground is unchanged. A walker and a tractor standing at the same off-road point receive the same surface factor. Update the playing guide, which currently says snow does not slow walking.

## Planned — Make a landing possible

Taking off works. Landing on the public strip, with the keyboard, does not. A careful pilot who has already flown the circuit still loses the aircraft on touchdown. The one landing the game itself can complete is a test pilot in `tests/flight.test.ts`: ten times a second it recomputes the exact pitch for a chosen sink rate, on an infinite flat plane, and taps the stick only when the error exceeds about two degrees. That is a closed-loop program. It is not the way the keys behave, and it never has to hit the runway.

**What the keys actually do.** Throttle (W/S) is a setting. It moves at 0.24 per second, so idle to full, or full back to idle, takes about four seconds. In the air, S only turns the throttle down. Nose and wings are rates, not positions (`fly` in `src/shared/flight.ts`). Holding an arrow pitches at about twenty degrees a second near approach speed, and the rotation keeps going for a fraction of a second after the key comes up. Releasing the bank keys leaves the wings where they are. Releasing the pitch keys only weakly settles the nose toward the flight path. The guide tells you to use the opposite key to level the wings. B is read only while the wheels are on the ground, where it is a wheel brake of about 5.5 m/s². In the air it does nothing. There is no airbrake, spoiler or flap.

**What counts as a crash, checked at the instant the wheels meet the ground.** All of these destroy the airframe. The notice names the reason, and the pilot is put down on foot, injured.

- Sink faster than **3.5 m/s**. The test pilot aims for 1.4 m/s, then 0.6 m/s in the last 4 m, which is five times gentler than the limit. A flare that is a fraction of a second late crosses 3.5. The climb gauge is marked ±10 m/s with no landing mark, so 3.5 sits close to the centre and looks ordinary.
- The sink figure is not only the aircraft's vertical speed. It is `(support - ground) / dt - vy`. Any rise in the ground between two physics steps is added in full. On the flat this is zero. On a slope it is not.
- Nose below **−5.7°** (`pitch < -0.1`), however soft the sink. That is still the attitude of an approach that has not flared yet. Nose above **+20°** is a tail strike. That whole safe band is about a second of holding the arrow.
- Bank past **12°**. That is about a third of a second of an arrow key, or whatever bank was left from turning onto the strip.
- Speed past **36 m/s (about 81 mph)**, or the direction of travel differing from the nose by more than **20°**. Both are reported as one phrase, "uncontrolled landing". The 81 mph cap is the loose one. The 20° of slip is the one a turn onto final leaves behind.
- After the wheels are down, faster than 8 m/s, if the ground 4 m to either side is more than 1.2 m above the wheels: "wing strike on terrain".

`approachSpeed` (24 m/s, about 54 mph) is the number the guide and the test talk about. `fly` never reads it. Nothing holds the aircraft at that speed.

**Why the runway does not save you.** Puddlewick's marked strip is **240 m by 18 m** (220 m where a new parish has the smaller town). The level grade extends 10 m past the paint, and then a **3 m** blend (`airfieldHeight`) joins ground that the siting rules allow to be **2 m** higher or lower. A wheel rolling that blend at landing speed sees a rise steep enough, through the formula above, to exceed 3.5 m/s by itself. Running off the end onto lower ground unsticks the wheels (`support` more than 0.15 m below the strip) and the fall back down is another landing check. The ground roll after a correct touchdown is short — on the order of 50 m from 55 mph with the wheel brake — so 240 m is enough pavement once the wheels are down in the first half. The misses happen in the arrival, and on the shoulder, before the length is used up. Eight destroyed aircraft is what these limits produce.

**How to make a landing a thing a person can do.** Keep the same aircraft. Widen the arrival, give the air a way to slow down, and make the strip a larger, gentler target. Tune the numbers below in play; they are the starting point.

1. **Survive a firm, slightly crooked arrival.** Raise the destructive sink to about **8 m/s**. Allow about **15° nose-down** when the sink is inside that limit, and about **25° nose-up**. Allow about **25° of bank** and about **40° of slip**. Keep the speed cap near 80 mph, or move it to about 100 mph, and say "too fast" and "sideways" as separate notices. A dive (around 10 m/s of sink), a steep bank (around 45°), water and buildings still destroy the aircraft. Judge the sink from the aircraft's vertical speed against the surface under a wheelbase, so a slope the wheels can roll is a roll and a sudden rise is the only one that counts as impact.
2. **An airbrake on the key people already hold.** Holding B in the air adds drag: enough to descend at about 2–4 m/s around approach speed without pushing the nose down, and to bleed a fast arrival back toward that speed. It must not be able to stall an aircraft that is already slow with the nose level. On the ground, B stays the wheel brake. Throttle can stay a four-second lever; the brake is what makes "reduce power and come down" possible on the way in, instead of only after the wheels are on the ground.
3. **A released stick should be a steady approach.** Slow the keyboard pitch and roll so a tap is a few degrees, and let the nose and the wings ease back toward the flight path when the keys are up. Analog sticks can keep more authority. This is stability, not an automatic landing. There should still be no "press a key to land".
4. **A longer, wider, softer strip.** Lengthen the public runway toward **400 m** and widen the pavement toward **30 m** where the town site allows; if 400 m does not fit, take the longest level site that does, and still widen it. Stretch the blend from 3 m to about **15–20 m** so the shoulder is a slope, not a ramp. Length is the smaller half of this item. The contact rules and the shoulder are why a longer strip alone would still wreck the aircraft.

**Per-world.** The sink, bank, pitch, slip and speed limits, whether the airbrake exists, and the public runway's length and width are world rules. The default is this easier set, including on Puddlewick. A world that wants today's limits can select them. The old numbers stay available as that strict option; they stop being what a new pilot meets.

**Tests.** A landing flown the way the keys work — full presses, not the optimal controller — on the real graded runway survives a late flare, a few degrees of bank and a few degrees of nose-down, and stops on the pavement. Rolling off the paint onto the blend at landing speed does not by itself destroy the aircraft. The airbrake increases descent and reduces speed above the approach, does not stall a level aircraft already at that speed, and remains the wheel brake on the ground. A 10 m/s dive, a bank around 45°, water and a building still destroy the airframe. Update the flight tests that currently require the 3.5 m/s, 12° and −5.7° cuts, so those assertions are not a hidden demand to keep the old limits.

## Planned — Flight view: terrain squares, chunky ground and distant towns

Seen from the biplane over open country, in clear weather, at about 66 mph and 20–30 frames per second. On foot the same world looks continuous. From the air three things show at once.

- **A hard square of ground around the aircraft.** The land directly under the plane is a sharper, speckled meadow. A straight edge, aligned to the world grid, cuts that off from the surrounding hills. Past the edge the ground is smoother, blotchier and made of large flat facets. The square moves with the aircraft and does not follow the shape of the hill.
- **Chunky, half-loaded ground farther out.** Hills break into big triangular faces. Patches look unfinished, as if a coarser stand-in is still on screen while the real surface has not caught up. Trees and small ground cover appear and disappear in blocks rather than thinning out with distance.
- **Towns at a distance look like a lump, not a town.** A settlement on the horizon reads as a pale geometric pad with a few thin shapes on it, sitting on land that does not match the hills around it. Roads, yards and building colours that are obvious up close are missing. The pad has its own outline, separate from the terrain.

**Why the ground is two different surfaces.** The island is too large for one mesh, so `GroundTiles` in `src/client/ground.ts` builds 128 m squares around the player and drops squares left behind. Each square is one of two kinds (`groundGeometrySteps` in `src/client/ground-geometry.ts`, and the surface mask in `groundMaterial`):

- Near squares, whose centre is within 560 m **on the ground** (kept until 680 m), are a 4 m grid (2 m on cliffs and rivers) with a 256-pixel surface mask, so grass, paths and soil stay readable. They can cast shadows. Height is ignored: `d` in `GroundTiles.update` is `Math.hypot(cx - x, cz - z)`, and play calls it with `this.target.x` and `this.target.z` only. A square under the aircraft is "near" at any altitude.
- Far squares, out to about 1 km, are 16 m cells fanned from the middle of each cell, with a 64-pixel mask. That is enough to hold the shape of a hill and cheap enough to keep the horizon filled, and the shared edge samples are there so the two kinds meet without a crack in height.

The crack is hidden. The change in cell size and in texture size is not. From the ground you are inside the near region and the far squares sit out toward the fog. From the biplane the camera looks across the grid, so the straight edge of a 128 m square is the thing you see. At 66 mph the aircraft also outruns the builder: play only finishes about two squares per frame, and only inside a few milliseconds (`ground.update` budget), keeping the old square on screen until the new one is done. The detailed patch therefore lags behind the aircraft and the border sits close to the wings instead of a comfortable distance ahead.

**Why the distance looks unloaded.** Woodland, outcrops and gathering grounds stream in separate 256 m chunks (`src/client/countryside.ts`), only out to about 480 m, and only one chunk is built at a time. Beyond the ground squares, a single low-detail island mesh (`src/client/horizon-land.ts`) fills the view. In clear weather the fog does not hide it: `marineVisibility` is 45 km when the sky is clear, 14 km in precipitation, 4.5 km in a storm. That mesh uses roughly 64 m cells, but any cell near a building or a headland is rebuilt at about 8 m. Open country stays the coarse fan. Where a streamed ground square exists, the horizon mesh is punched out. A town that the ground squares have not reached yet is therefore an island of finer facets on a coarse green sheet, with the real building models standing on it and none of the road and yard colouring. That is the pale pad.

**This is a client graphics problem, not a world rule.** It follows the existing graphics choice (performance, adaptive, detailed). A world owner should not have a slider for it. Making every square detailed would remove the border and drop the frame rate further; these screenshots are already at 20–30 fps.

**Ways to address it, cheapest first.** Do not start by raising detail everywhere.

1. **Give flight a shorter clear view.** While airborne, pull the fog in so the coarse island mesh and the unfinished squares are not examined at 45 km. The square under the wings would remain at low level, because it is nearby. This is the smallest change and the one to try first.
2. **Count height the same way as ground distance.** A square 400 m below the aircraft is as far from the camera as a square 400 m ahead at eye level, and should take the coarse mesh. Compare the existing 560 / 680 m test with slant range, `Math.hypot(cx - x, height, cz - z)`, instead of the flat ground distance. On the ground nothing changes. As the aircraft climbs, the detailed disc shrinks, and once height itself passes the radius every loaded square is coarse, so the border is gone and the builder stops spending frames on a 4 m mesh nobody can read. Keep the hysteresis so climbing and descending do not flicker. Two limits. At the height in these screenshots the ground under the wings is still close: a 16 m facet subtends several degrees, and forcing it coarse would replace the border with a uniformly chunky meadow. And a landing needs the fine mesh back before the wheels are low, prioritized ahead of other squares, or the runway stays coarse while the builder catches up. A scale on the height term can decide how soon the disc shrinks; a pure slant range is the direct reading of "the same as a far square."
3. **Keep a ring of near-quality squares ahead of the aircraft.** Spend the existing time budget on the squares in front of the velocity, and start upgrading a square before the camera can see its edge. The 4 m / 16 m and 256 / 64 jumps stay, but the border moves out to where it is smaller on screen. Measure the frame time in level flight before widening the budget; one cliff square already spreads across many frames on purpose. This is the part that still matters at circuit height, where slant range leaves the square under the wings detailed.
4. **Softening the step between the two square types.** An intermediate grid (about 8 m) and a shared surface-mask size, or a narrow cross-fade on the square edge, would remove the inked line without a new terrain system. Edge heights must still match, which the current shared edge samples are for.
5. **One resolution that changes with distance** (a clipmap, or several rings rather than a yes/no flag per 128 m square). This is the real fix for the border and the largest piece of work. It replaces `GroundTiles`'s two-kind switch and has to keep the no-crack edge rule, snow, rivers and cliffs.
6. **Stop the distant town being a special pad.** Use one cell size for the horizon mesh, including under buildings, and fade building models out with the same fog as the land. Stream ground squares toward a town the player is flying at before its horizon pad is the thing on screen.

**Tests.** A flight-speed path over open ground should not leave a detailed/coarse border inside a set distance of the aircraft for more than a short time. At a height past the detail radius, every loaded square is coarse, including the one under the aircraft, and a descent brings the fine mesh back under the aircraft before touchdown height. On the ground the 560 / 680 m disc is unchanged. Adjacent squares must still share edge heights. Frame time in level flight at the performance graphics setting must stay within an agreed budget. A distant town, before its ground squares arrive, should not read as a separate plateau.

## Planned — Solid trees, boulders and vehicles

- **Problem:** much of the landscape can be driven straight through. Movement (`move` in `src/shared/simulation.ts`) only tests cliffs, inland water, buildings, parked trailers, creator-placed barriers and landscape fences; it never tests trees, boulders, scenery rocks, resource-ground stones or other players. Trees and rocks are generated **on the client only** (`src/client/countryside.ts` seeds each streamed chunk and `src/client/scenery.ts` scatters the village surroundings, with `countryside` counts that differ between low and normal quality), so the server does not know where a tree is and cannot stop anyone at it. Players and their vehicles likewise pass through each other.
- **Shared, deterministic obstacles:** move tree and boulder placement into a shared module that both server and client call: a per-chunk seeded generator producing `{x, z, kind, radius, height}` for every solid object, independent of render quality (quality may skip drawing some, never place different ones), honouring the same exclusions as today (roads, buildings and construction, fields, pastures, gathering grounds, airfields, spaceport fence, water). Cache per chunk with a spatial hash so `move()` can query obstacles near a swept segment in constant time, invalidate when buildings or roads change, and include coastal rocks (`src/client/coastal-rocks.ts`), scenery rocks and the boulders at stone gathering grounds. Trunks block with a small radius so a tractor fits between most trees in open woodland but not through a dense northern forest; crowns do not block. Creator scatter already has a `solid` flag; keep that path and make the default procedural scenery solid in the same way.
- **Movement response:** collision with a tree or boulder stops the vehicle (speed to zero, as the cliff and inland-water branches do) with no damage, condition loss or fuel penalty; walking players slide around round obstacles rather than sticking. Keep `allowEscape` behaviour so anyone who loads inside a newly solid tree can drive out. Boats are unaffected except for coastal rocks they already avoid via cliffs.
- **Vehicle-to-vehicle collision:** treat every other player on land as an oriented footprint (the vehicle's `length` × `width`, or the walking `collisionRadius`) and test the swept segment against it. **No damage** to either party. Which vehicle stops depends on the geometry of the impact: a vehicle that drives into a stationary or slower one stops itself and the other is unaffected; a head-on closing between two moving vehicles stops both; a glancing contact below a shallow angle slides along the other hull and continues at reduced speed. There is no push, shove or bounce, so a parked tractor cannot be rammed off a road and a stationary player is never moved by someone else's input. Walking players cannot pass through a tractor but can step around it, and a tractor cannot drive through a pedestrian (it stops short), which also stops the existing silent overlap of two riders on one spot.
- **Prediction and fairness:** the server owns the outcome. Client prediction tests against the latest snapshot positions of other players, accepts small corrections on contact, and never predicts another player's stop. Decide what counts as an obstacle when its owner is offline or at home: an offline player's vehicle persisting at its last position becomes a permanent obstacle, so either hide it from collision after sign-off or apply the parked-trailer rules. Add an anti-griefing rule for vehicles left across a narrow route (gates, bridges, jetties, the arrival area): the existing arrival-area and town-centre clearances should refuse stopping there, and a vehicle stationary with its driver offline for a period should not block.
- **Per-world:** rules for solid scenery (off, trunks only, trunks and boulders), vehicle-to-vehicle collision (off, stop-only, with the glancing slide), pedestrian blocking, the offline-vehicle obstacle timeout and the glancing-angle threshold, so arenas and sandbox worlds can keep today's pass-through behaviour.
- **Scope and performance:** the obstacle query must stay cheap at the enlarged island's density (hundreds of trees per chunk, several moving players); test the per-tick cost with `scripts/island-performance.ts`. Road growth, NPC ground navigation and construction previews should consult the same obstacle data so NPCs do not path through trunks and new roads are not grown through a solid boulder. Snow accumulation already treats trees as `snowObstacle`; reuse the same objects.
- **Tests:** identical obstacle sets for the same chunk at both quality levels and across server/client; a tractor stopped by a trunk and a boulder without condition loss; walking slide-around; escape from inside an obstacle; rear-end, head-on and glancing vehicle contacts with the correct vehicle(s) stopping and no position change for the struck party; pedestrian versus tractor; offline-owner rule; no regression in the northern-island movement tests (fords, deep reaches) or road growth; frame and tick budgets unchanged.

## Planned — Earthmoving machines and a building slope limit

Two connected pieces: a stricter, size-aware slope rule for construction, and late-game bulldozers and backhoes that can cut and fill the ground to make level pads, used by their owners or hired out to other players. The slope rule gives earthmoving a purpose; earthmoving gives the slope rule an answer.

### What exists today

- `foundationProfile` in `src/shared/construction-site.ts` samples a footprint every two metres and `constructionSiteReason` refuses any site whose rise exceeds **2 m**, regardless of building size ("Ground rises more than 2 metres across this footprint"). A 4 m shed is allowed on a 45% slope while a 30 m hall is refused on a 6% one. Completed buildings store `foundationY` and keep their datum.
- Terrain is a function, not a grid: `terrainHeight` in `src/shared/terrain.ts` composes the village, countryside, northern island, creator heightmap, world-owner **terrain stamps** (`w.terrain`, additive cones with radius 1–100 m and height ±30 m, at most 256 per world, placed by the `terrain` action, owner only), coastal bluffs, airfield and spaceport grading, and the seabed. Collision, water, fords, roads, construction, NPC navigation, the map worker and the rendered chunks all read it, so any earthwork that changes `terrainHeight` propagates everywhere by construction. `airfieldHeight` and `spaceportGround` already show the shape wanted: a flat pad with smooth shoulders blended into the natural ground.
- Spoil goods already exist (`topsoil`, `gravel`, `stone`, `sand`), as does the quarry tipping trailer (1,000 weight of those goods) and the `excavator` gathering skill name, which this work must not collide with.

### Slope limit for construction

- Replace the flat 2 m rule with a **grade** rule: allowed rise scales with the footprint span, for example at most ~12% grade along either footprint axis (rise ÷ span) plus a small absolute allowance so tiny buildings are not stricter than today, with an absolute ceiling (the current 2 m) for anything. Keep it lenient: ordinary village lots, gentle hillsides and river terraces remain buildable without machinery; steep valley sides, ridge noses and mountain slopes are not. Fields and pasture parcels get their own, slightly more lenient grade check; roads keep their existing cost-based grade handling.
- The refusal message quantifies the problem and the remedy: "Ground rises 3.4 m (17%) across this footprint; level it to within 1.8 m with earthworks, or choose a flatter site". The build-menu site plan shades the footprint by how far each sample is above or below the achievable pad level, so a player can see where cut and fill are needed before hiring a machine.
- Existing buildings are never re-validated; the new rule applies to new placements, expansions and relocations only. Make the grade thresholds world rules (Editor → Rules → Construction) with Puddlewick defaults, and give the world owner a bypass since they can already stamp terrain.

### Earthworks data model

- Add `w.earthworks`: a list of **pads**, each an oriented rectangle (or a short polygon) with a target level, a shoulder width and maximum shoulder grade, an owner, a creation time, a progress fraction and a surface finish (bare earth → gravel → grass as it weathers). `terrainHeight` applies pads after stamps and before bluffs/airfield grading, blending natural ground to the target level across the shoulder exactly as `spaceportGround` does, scaled by progress so a half-finished pad is visibly half dug. Cache pads in a spatial grid; `terrainHeight` is hot and must not scan every pad for every sample.
- Limits: a maximum pad size (for example 60 × 60 m) and shoulder, a maximum cut or fill depth (for example 6 m), a per-world pad count and total area, and a rule that pads may not overlap buildings, roads, fields, pastures, gathering grounds, town centres and arrival areas, no-build zones, the airfield, the spaceport reservation, coastal bluffs, inland water and its banks, or another player's unfinished pad. Pads may overlap and supersede earlier **completed** pads by the same owner (re-grading). Rivers and ponds cannot be filled or diverted in this slice.
- Pads persist in snapshots and portable designs, survive restarts, and are included in the terrain revision used by the map worker and streamed chunks so both rebuild the affected area. Trees and boulders inside a pad and its shoulder are removed by the shared obstacle generator (see the solid scenery item) once progress passes the point where the ground under them has changed; the gathering-ground and woodland exclusions already handle similar cases.

### Machines

- Two vehicle definitions, both **manufactured only at the automotive factory** (see the haulage programme) and therefore unavailable until that industry exists; prices on the order of a tractor-trailer, with heavy fuel burn:
  - **Bulldozer** — tracked, slow (around 8 km/h, much slower off any surface than a tractor), wide front blade, large volume per pass, best for pushing cut material across a pad into fill. Cannot tow. Modelled in detail: tracks that animate and leave the existing tyre-track system's marks as plates, blade lift, cab with human-scale operator, lights, exhaust stack.
  - **Backhoe / excavator** — wheeled, faster on roads, front loader bucket and rear digging arm; smaller volume per cycle but precise, can load its spoil into an adjacent tipping trailer and can dig below the pad for trenches in a later slice. Animated boom, stick, bucket and stabiliser legs when working.
- Operating either requires a new **plant operator** qualification learned at school (distinct from the existing `excavator` gathering skill and the `driver` licence), counted against the skill limit like other licences, and required regardless of the world's `vehicleLicences` toggle in Puddlewick; driving one onto a road without the skill is refused the same way as other licence checks.

### How the work is done

1. **Survey.** The owner (or a client, see contracting) opens **Earthworks** from the machine's panel or the build menu, draws a pad on the site plan (size, rotation, target level clamped between the footprint's lowest and highest natural points plus the depth limits), and sees the computed **cut volume**, **fill volume**, net spoil or shortfall, shoulder extent, estimated passes and fuel, and any placement refusal. Nothing is charged until the pad is confirmed; confirming reserves the area (no construction there until the pad completes or is abandoned).
2. **Working passes.** The operator drives the machine inside the pad with the blade or bucket engaged (a key or button, like trawling or the flashlight). Progress accrues only while the machine is moving slowly within the pad with the implement down; each metre travelled moves a volume set by the machine, the material (topsoil is cheap to move, rock-bearing upland costs several times more, read from the local resource province) and the operator's condition/fuel. Progress is a fraction of total volume so the ground morphs gradually and visibly. The server owns progress; the client previews the blade lowering and dust.
3. **Material balance.** Cut material first supplies fill within the same pad. Net surplus goes into the machine's spoil hopper (bulldozer: a berm pushed to the pad edge represented as a small stamp; backhoe: real goods in a bucket) and from there into a tipping trailer or a spoil heap, producing saleable `topsoil`/`gravel`/`stone` at a conversion rate below quarrying so earthworks is not a better mine than a quarry. Net shortfall must be delivered as `gravel` or `topsoil` to the pad before the last fill passes can complete, using the ordinary trailer and inventory transfer flows; the panel shows what remains to be delivered.
4. **Completion and weathering.** When progress reaches 1 the pad is level to within a tolerance (for example ±0.15 m) and the reservation turns into a buildable surface that passes the grade rule. ## Planned — Player-built airstrips and commercial aviation

The purpose is intercity air travel once towns exist far across the expanded island: a player builds an airstrip near a distant settlement, buys passenger or cargo aircraft, and runs flights between airstrips for a fare or a freight margin. Today there is exactly one public airfield (`w.airfield`, a single north/south reservation with one hangar, `src/shared/airfield.ts`) and one aircraft type, the biplane (slot 2, 90 weight hold).

### Buildable airstrip

- Add an **airstrip** building kind, placed through the ordinary build menu with the usual five architectural variants, finishes, rotation and site plan. Its footprint is a graded runway strip plus an apron with a small hangar/office crib; the runway should be long enough for every planned aircraft's takeoff and landing run with margin (the biplane's ground roll is roughly 150–200 m once the wheels are already down; arriving on the present strip is the separate problem in "Make a landing possible". Larger aircraft will need more, so size the strip to the heaviest planned type, likely 400–600 m by 30 m, and decide whether two strip lengths are offered).
- Make it **extremely expensive**, well beyond a bridge (about 800s target) and the boathouse (18s): a starting target on the order of **2,000–3,000s** plus very large material quantities drawn from goods that already exist — gravel and concrete for the strip base and apron, steel and wood or logs for the hangar, blocks for the office — with final figures calculated against the economy so it is a late-game community investment rather than something one player buys early. Support material contribution by several players and construction helpers like other large sites, with persistent unfinished progress and the existing cancellation refund rules.
- Siting rules: the whole rotated strip must be dry ground outside protected areas, other premises, fields, pastures, roads and inland water, with limited slope end to end and across; the construction preview must show the graded profile and refuse sites that need more than a bounded cut/fill. Grading should reuse the `airfieldHeight` approach so the runway is flat and terrain, collision, trees and rendering agree; trees inside the strip and an approach cone at each end are cleared, and later construction inside the approach cones is refused.
- Generalise the single `w.airfield` reservation into a list of airfields with arbitrary rotation and an owner, and make `onAirfield`, `onRunway`, `airfieldHeight`, road routing, farm/pasture placement, resource placement and the map honour every entry. The parish map shows airstrips with an icon and name, and the parish directory lists them with distance so pilots can plan a route.
- Runway lighting and a windsock are rendered detail; lighting matters for night flights and should be visible from the air at distance like the lighthouses.

### Additional aircraft types

- Add several new aircraft definitions in `data/vehicles.json` sharing `src/shared/flight.ts` dynamics with per-type mass, wing area, drag, thrust, stall speed, fuel burn and takeoff/landing distances, each modelled in detail with cockpit/cabin interiors at human scale. A starting set: a **light passenger aircraft** (4–6 passengers, modest cabin baggage), a **larger passenger aircraft** (8–12 passengers, needs the long strip), a **light utility/cargo aircraft** (a few hundred weight), a **cargo aircraft** (roughly 1,000–1,500 weight, comparable to one or two tipping trailers, needs the long strip), and the **seaplane** in "Seaplanes" (pilot and two passengers, about 400 weight, runway and open water). Prices should scale sharply with capacity; the cargo aircraft and large passenger aircraft are late-game purchases.
- Cargo capacity is a hard **weight** limit, not a slot count, using the existing per-item weights, so heavy goods (fish, stone, ore) fill a hold fast while light goods pack well. Loaded weight changes performance: longer ground run, slower climb, higher stall speed, more fuel; an overloaded aircraft cannot be loaded, not merely warned. Fuel is bought from a hangar/airstrip in the same way as boats buy from a boatyard, and a strip's owner may stock fuel for sale.
- Loading and unloading happen only parked on an apron and use the ordinary carry/transfer flows; decide whether aircraft can trade directly with a shop within range (like trailers) or only through the pilot's inventory. Define parking: an aircraft shut down on an apron stays visible and persists like a moored boat; an aircraft abandoned on open ground outside any strip is either refused or recovered to the nearest strip for a fee.
- Keep existing biplane behaviour, the Boatmaster/pilot licence rules, crash destruction of the active airframe and the authoritative crash notice unchanged; the new types extend the same code paths and tests.

### Passenger flights as a business

- A pilot with a passenger aircraft offers **flights**: announce a destination airstrip and fare, accept boarding passengers at an airstrip, fly, and settle on arrival. Reuse the charter boat's invitation, ticket and fare-escrow model (`src/shared/charter*.ts`) so money is held when the passenger boards and paid to the pilot when the passenger disembarks at a strip, with refunds if the pilot cancels or the flight never leaves. Walk-up boarding at a strip and a parish directory listing of flights on offer both need designing; avoid requiring a passenger to be in chat with the pilot.
- Passengers ride in a cabin seat with a window view, cannot control the aircraft, and may leave only on the ground at a strip (or by the existing rules for leaving a vehicle on the ground elsewhere; never in flight). A passenger may carry limited baggage: a small weight allowance per seat (on the order of 20–30 weight, well below a trailer and a fraction of the hold), enforced at boarding against what the passenger is carrying; excess must be left behind or shipped as cargo.
- A **crash strands everyone aboard**. Nobody dies. The airframe is destroyed, and the pilot and every passenger are set down on foot at the crash. Each person keeps what they can carry on foot; the rest of the cargo, including hold freight and baggage that will not fit, stays at the site. Walking away leaves it there. Collecting it means driving a vehicle back to the crash. A notice names the flight, the pilot and the place, in the same style as the existing crash notice. Escrowed fares for a crashed flight are refunded, not paid.
- Mixed loads: a passenger aircraft may carry small cargo in a hold, and a cargo aircraft may carry a very small number of passengers only if its definition says so; the weight limit is shared.
- NPC residents should eventually be able to buy flights to move between towns when that makes economic sense for them, using the same tickets; this is a later slice.

### Air freight economics

- Cargo flights are meant to profit from **price differences between developing towns** (the existing per-town business prices and local shortages from the northern resource provinces). Fuel burn per kilometre, aircraft price, maintenance and the hard weight cap set the margin; calibrate so a full cargo aircraft on a long route beats a trailer only where roads are poor or distances are long, and never beats a cargo boat or truck on bulk goods per unit cost. Document the expected margins per route length in HAULAGE.md once tuned.
- Air freight interacts with spoilage and perishables (fish), supply intents and parish supply orders; confirm deliveries by air count like any other delivery.
- **Per-world:** airstrip cost and materials, strip length options, approach-cone size, landing-fee and parking policy, whether private strips are open to all, aircraft prices, capacities, seat counts and performance through the vehicle editor, passenger baggage allowance, fare escrow and refund rules, stranding everyone aboard on a passenger-flight crash, and whether NPC residents buy flights.

## Planned — Flight combat for fighting worlds

Air combat for worlds that enable fighting: biplanes and later aircraft types carry guns and other weapons, dogfight, strafe and bomb, inside the existing arena modes and in open fighting worlds. Nothing here applies to a world with fighting off, and every rule is a per-world setting.

### What exists today

Turrets exist as a building kind.
- Flight: `src/shared/flight.ts` runs substepped lift/drag/thrust dynamics with server-owned crash outcomes, local prediction and remote interpolation of pitch and bank; a crash destroys only the active airframe and injures the pilot. The biplane is the only aircraft; the aviation item above adds more types.

### Aircraft weapons and damage

- Add an **aircraft armament** model: hardpoints per aircraft type (the biplane: two fixed forward machine guns synchronised through the propeller arc and a small bomb rack; later types: more guns, rockets, a turret position for a gunner passenger), each with a weapon from a new aircraft weapon table (fixed guns with convergence distance, unguided rockets, light bombs with a fall ballistic and blast, and optionally a rear gunner). Ammunition is finite per sortie and reloaded at a hangar or airstrip for a fee; in energy-mode worlds the existing regenerating energy applies to guns only.
- Give aircraft an **airframe health** separate from the pilot: hits reduce the airframe, degrade performance progressively (engine power loss with smoke, control damage that biases roll or pitch, fuel leak draining the tank, a fire that counts down to structural failure), and at zero the aircraft breaks up and the existing crash path runs. The pilot can also be hit directly through the cockpit with a low probability per gun hit and dies under arena rules. Damage scales with the existing armour formula so the vehicle editor's armour field applies.
- Hit detection is server-authoritative and lag-compensated: fixed guns fire a short burst of fast projectiles along the aircraft's gun line (reusing the projectile code and the arena's safe-zone and protection checks), with target hulls tested as oriented boxes using the aircraft's length, span and the server's rewound positions for the shooter's reported latency within a bound. Bombs and rockets reuse blast falloff against ground targets, vehicles and turrets. Tracers, muzzle flash, hit sparks, smoke trails and debris are client effects driven by server events.

## Planned — Helicopters: sling loads, sea rescue and passenger charters

A helicopter is an aircraft that can hover, land anywhere flat, and do three things no aeroplane can: carry a tractor in a sling beneath it, winch a stranded boater up a rope ladder and set them down again, and give people lifts from wherever they stand. All three can be offered as paid jobs at rates the customer agrees to.

### What exists today

- Flight is fixed-wing only. `src/shared/flight.ts` integrates `FlightState { throttle, pitch, bank, vx, vy, vz, airspeed, aoa, stalled, grounded }` with a stall angle, rotation and approach speeds and landing sink/bank/pitch limits (`FLIGHT`), and `fly()` returns an accident reason that destroys the active airframe. There is no hover: lift comes from airspeed, so a helicopter cannot reuse `fly()` unchanged. The biplane is slot 2, boarded only at the hangar apron (`commissionBiplane`, `hasAircraftHangar`); the planned aircraft types in "Player-built airstrips and commercial aviation" all share this fixed-wing model.
- Passengers and riders: `hitch` makes a player a passenger of another's vehicle (`p.hitch = driverId`, positioned by `charterPassengerPosition` on the charter boat and crew berths on the trawler); the charter boat (`src/shared/charter.ts`) already has **offers → tickets → fare escrow → settlement** (`CharterOffer { fare, minutes, dock }`, `settleCharterPassenger`, refunds on cancel/death), which the aviation section plans to reuse for flights.
- Stranding and rescue: a powered boat with `fuel <= 0` on water can launch the lifeboat (`rescueAction` in `src/shared/boat-rescue.ts`), which records the abandoned hull in `w.boatRescues[p.id]` with position, heading, paint, slot and cargo; another player can tow it (`src/shared/towing.ts`) or give the owner a recovery ride (`hitch` is allowed to a rescuer when `boatRescues[p.id]` exists); `refuellingStatus`/`refuelPlayer` in `src/shared/player-aid.ts` let a nearby player pour fuel into someone else's tank. Nobody can be lifted off a boat.
- Vehicles are a mode of the player (see the parked-vehicles section): a tractor has no position of its own to pick up, so the sling load depends on `parkedVehicles` existing first, or at minimum on the tractor-under-tow being represented as a world object for the duration.

### The aircraft

- New vehicle slot **helicopter** (`data/vehicles.json`, mode air, with a `rotorcraft: true` flag that `vehicleDef` exposes): a light utility helicopter with one pilot seat and **three passenger seats**, a small internal **cargo hold of 60 weight** (less than walking's 60-plus and far below the biplane's 90, so it is a people-and-tools machine, not a freighter), a 90-unit tank, roughly 110 mph cruise, and a price around **the biplane's × 2.5** (calculated in the balance test). Requires the `pilot` licence in licence worlds. Bought and boarded at the hangar like the biplane, parked on any flat spot (a 6 m radius at under the building slope limit, not water) when the parked-vehicles section exists, otherwise returned to the hangar apron on dismount with the same apron-occupancy rules.
- **Flight model** in a new `src/shared/rotorcraft.ts` sharing `FlightEnvironment` and the crash/terrain/blocked checks: collective (climb/descend) on the throttle axis, cyclic pitch and roll on the stick, pedals on the rudder keys; lift is independent of airspeed with a hover ceiling, translational lift adding a little above 20 mph, a vortex-ring warning and sink when descending fast with no airspeed, a hard landing limit (vertical speed and bank) that destroys the airframe like a fixed-wing crash, and auto-rotation when fuel runs out (steep controlled descent; survivable only on a flat landing). Hover hold: holding a key trims to zero velocity so winching and slinging are not a dexterity test. Mobile gets the same two-stick layout as the biplane with collective on a slider.
- **Model**: a bubble-canopy light helicopter with skids, tail boom and rotor (main and tail rotor spin with the engine, blur discs above idle), a cargo hook under the belly, a winch arm and rope ladder on the right door, cabin interior with four seats at human scale (first-person pilot view through the canopy), nav lights and a landing light that uses the vehicle-lights code; downwash flattens grass and ripples water within 12 m (shader uniform, as the hovercraft does) and blows snow.

### Sling loading a tractor

- With the hook lowered (`hookDown` toggle) and the helicopter hovering within 2 m horizontally and 6–10 m above a **parked tractor** (any owned-fleet slot that `isTractor`), the pilot presses the interact key to **attach**: the tractor's parked record gains `slung: helicopterId`, it is drawn hanging from a 7 m cable with pendulum swing driven by the helicopter's acceleration, and the helicopter's mass rises (slower climb, lower ceiling, longer stopping) by a per-slot `mass` added to `VehicleDef` (tractors roughly 1,500–3,000 kg by capacity; there is no mass field today). Only one load; trailers must be unhitched first; cargo aboard the tractor stays aboard and counts toward the sling weight limit (**tractor + 200 weight**, so a loaded Barley Dray will not lift). Over water the load may be released only above 1 m of ground or the load is lost (sinks: the tractor record moves to the boat-rescue-style abandoned list and must be recovered by a tug for a fee, a strong incentive to be careful).
- **Set down**: hover under 1.5 m above flat ground (slope under the building limit, not water, not a road if the world forbids parking there) and release; the tractor becomes a parked vehicle at that point with its fuel and condition unchanged. Owners can therefore have their own stranded, out-of-fuel tractor flown back to town, or a hauler can pay to have a tractor moved across a river that has no bridge yet.
- Picking up **someone else's** tractor requires their consent through the contract flow below (an accepted "airlift" contract names the vehicle, pickup and drop-off); without it the hook refuses with "That tractor belongs to Hank — offer an airlift contract".

### Sea rescue by rope ladder

- Hovering under 12 m above a **stranded boater** (a player whose boat is on water with `fuel <= 0`, or who is drifting in a lifeboat with a `boatRescues` entry) with the **ladder deployed**, the pilot offers a lift; the boater sees "Grab the rope ladder" and accepts. A 6-second winch animation plays (human figure climbs the ladder, pulled up as it retracts) during which both players are immobile, then the boater becomes a passenger (`hitch = pilotId`, seat 1–3). Their boat stays where it is: a powered boat becomes a `boatRescues` hull (the same record the lifeboat launch creates, so towing and the existing recovery-ride rules apply) and a lifeboat is left moored to it.
- The passenger is flown to land (any shore, a boathouse or the fishing pier), steps off, buys fuel, and the helicopter flies them **back**: hovering over their own abandoned hull with the ladder down offers "Climb down to your boat"; a reverse winch animation puts them aboard at the helm, they refuel from inventory with the existing `refuel` action and are under way. The ladder also works over a river dock, pier or boathouse landing for ordinary pick-ups.
- The helicopter cannot refuel the boat directly (no hose), cannot lift a boat, and cannot winch anyone in a storm above a wind threshold (the storm-capsizing section's wind figure) — the hover becomes too unstable and the offer is refused with that reason, so the lifeboat and tug remain the heavy-weather answer.

### Passenger lifts

- Any player standing outside (on foot, stopped, not at home or in a task) within 15 m of a landed helicopter — or under a hovering one with the ladder down — can be offered a seat. Passengers ride in the cabin seats with a window view, cannot control the aircraft, may carry what they are carrying (no baggage allowance beyond their own inventory, which is already weight-limited), and leave only when the helicopter is landed or hovering under 3 m with the ladder down. A crash strands everyone aboard under the aviation section's rules, with fares refunded. The pilot can set down at a named building, a map pin the passenger dropped, or wherever the passenger asks in chat; the panel shows the distance to the agreed destination and marks arrival when within 30 m.

### Contracts and charging

- All three services share one **contract** model in `src/shared/air-contracts.ts`, generalising the charter boat's offers and tickets: the pilot posts or a customer requests `{ kind: 'lift' | 'rescue' | 'airlift', pickup, destination?, fare, returnTrip?: boolean, expires }`; the other party **accepts the stated fare** (or counter-offers once), the fare is escrowed when the service starts (boarding, winching, or hooking), and paid on completion (set-down at the destination within 30 m, the boater back at their helm, or the tractor parked at the drop-off). Cancelling or crashing refunds; abandoning a passenger somewhere other than the destination refunds half and costs the pilot kudos. A pilot may also do any of these for free (fare 0) — friends and parish volunteers — and the parish may post a **standing rescue bounty** from the treasury so rescuing strangers pays even when the stranded player has no cash (configurable per world, default 2s on Puddlewick).
- Requests show up in a **Flights** directory (a tab beside the charter listings): stranded boaters' positions are listed automatically when they call for help ("Call for a helicopter" on the out-of-fuel boat panel, like the lifeboat button), tractor airlifts and lifts are posted by players, and NPC residents post lifts and airlifts when the planner finds them cheaper than the alternative (a resident whose tractor ran dry far from a pump will post an airlift at the going rate). NPC pilots are a later slice.

### Per-world rules, migration and tests

- Editor → Rules: helicopters available on/off, price and recipe (purchasable at the hangar, or only manufactured by the automotive factory in late-game worlds), passenger seats and hold weight, sling weight limit, winch wind limit, the rescue bounty and whether contracts are enforced (off lets any pilot hook any tractor — for cooperative or private worlds). Fighting worlds may arm the helicopter under the flight-combat section's rules in a later slice; not in scope here.
- No migration is needed; the new slot and contract records are additive. Depends on: the parked-vehicles section (for a tractor to be a thing with a position), and shares code with the airstrips/aviation section (hangars, parking, passenger seats and crash rules).
- Tests: rotorcraft hover, translational lift, vortex-ring sink, autorotation and hard-landing destruction; hook attach only over a parked tractor within the limits, sling weight refusal, release over water abandons the vehicle, set-down parks it; winch only at a stranded boater or landing under the wind limit, hull preserved as a `boatRescues` entry, return trip puts the boater at the helm; passenger boarding/leaving rules and crash deaths; contract escrow, settlement, counter-offer, refund and the rescue bounty; the directory lists stranded boaters automatically; browser tests for the sling pendulum, the ladder animation and the cockpit view.

## Planned — CB radios

- Add **CB radio** to vehicles: boats, tractors, aircraft, the future tractor-trailers and cargo boat, with an optional base station in buildings as a later slice. The radio is a separate microphone voice channel from the world-wide public chat, modelled on real 27 MHz citizens' band behaviour rather than as a chat room.
- Realistic simulation: **40 numbered channels**, one selected per radio, with channel 9 reserved for emergencies and channel 19 as the conventional road/haulage channel. **Push-to-talk**: the sender keys the microphone (a held key or button capturing actual microphone audio only while held, stopping on release and at the world-configured maximum duration), and **only one transmitter at a time can be received on a channel within range**. If two stations key simultaneously, listeners in range of both hear a garbled collision ("doubling"); a station that keys while another transmission is already being received is told the channel is busy and does not get through. Squelch hides weak signals; a volume/squelch control and a channel selector live in the vehicle dashboard, with keyboard, touch and controller bindings.
- Propagation: line-of-sight-ish range limited by distance and terrain, not world-wide. Starting targets: a few kilometres between vehicles on the ground, further from a hilltop or an aircraft, further still over water, with the mountain and ridges blocking; signal strength degrades audio progressively (noise and dropouts, then static) rather than cutting off at a hard radius. Compute reception per listener on the server from sender/receiver positions and a cheap terrain occlusion sample; the island at ten times its area is about 30 km across, so cross-island contact should require relays or aircraft, which gives aviation and hilltop stations a role.
- Radio messages are not stored in the chat log as public chat; show a radio pane with the active channel, received traffic with the sender's call sign (player name or an owner-set handle), signal strength and who is currently keyed. AI residents do not answer CB traffic initially. Radio participation follows the same player mute and `chatLocked` restrictions as public chat; audio is neither transcribed nor saved.
- Emergency use: channel 9 calls from a stranded or sinking vessel can be surfaced to nearby boats as a rescue prompt, tying into the existing lifeboat and fuel-rescue flows without adding automation.
- **Implementation:** a `radio` action (`key`, `release`, `channel`, `squelch`) handled in `src/shared/simulation.ts` with the server holding per-channel transmitter state (who is keyed, since when, session token, remaining on-air time) in a transient structure that is not saved; on each tick the server computes, for every listener with a radio on that channel, a signal strength from distance, a few terrain samples along the path and antenna height (vehicle type, altitude for aircraft), compares it with squelch, and relays bounded authenticated audio frames with per-listener reception strength; collisions arise naturally when two transmitters overlap in time and both exceed a listener's squelch. ## Planned — Butcher, meat and livestock life cycle

A butcher business closes the loop from pasture to plate: farmers raise animals that are born young and grow up, haul adults to the butcher, pay for slaughter and take away meat, which restaurants, inns, the Harbour stores and home kitchens turn into meals. Raw meat is never edible. All prices, times and limits are per-world rules.

### What exists today

- Four livestock businesses (`src/shared/livestock.ts`): dairy (cows → milk), sheepfold (sheep → wool), pig farm (pigs → compost) and henhouse (chickens → eggs), each with a minimum breeding stock, rations, pasture capacity and herd condition. **Arrange breeding** exists (two parents at 80% condition, four feed, four water, 20d, one hour, `breedingEnd`) and yields a tradable **adult** immediately; the `young` names (calf, lamb, piglet, chick) are defined but unused. Animals are stock items (`cows` weight 5, `pigs` 4, `sheep` 3, `chickens` 1) bought and sold at the farms and the Harbour stores, moved physically by the Meadow livestock wagon (six cattle-equivalents).

### Livestock life cycle

- Split each herd into **young** and **adult** stock: new items `calves`, `lambs`, `piglets`, `chicks` (weight below the adult, not sellable to the butcher, lower price). Breeding produces young, not adults. Young animals mature after a species-specific time measured in game days (starting targets: chicks 6 days, piglets 20, lambs 25, calves 40 — roughly 1 to 7 real hours at the 600 s day — tuned so a herd can double in a real week of good care), tracked per animal cohort on the building (`b.young: [{item, born, count}]`) and converted to adults by the ordinary building tick. - New building kind **butcher** (tier 0 or 1, five architectural variants: a stone slaughterhouse with a cold room, a timber village butcher's shop, a brick abattoir with a loading ramp, a whitewashed shop with a tiled front, a long low meat works), with a livestock loading ramp as its "waterfront"-style plan element so the wagon can back up to it. ## Planned — Snow ploughing: roads that stay snowed in until someone clears them

Roads should not clear themselves. Snow falls on roads as it does on fields, driving on an unploughed road is as slow as driving off-road, and the parish pays anyone with a plough blade by the distance they clear. While it keeps snowing, cleared roads fill in again and need another pass.

### What exists today

- Snow is a single world-wide number: `w.climate.snowDepth` (metres), advanced by `advanceClimate` in `src/shared/environment.ts` from `weather.snowfallPerDay` (0.018 m/day × storm strength, +0.15 in a blizzard; creator-forced snow 0.12 or 0.48) and melted by `NORMAL_STORM_SNOW × solarExposure` on clear days down to a `winterBase` of 4 cm. There is no per-road or per-place depth.
- Roads are implicitly ploughed. `drivingSurface` in `src/shared/driving-surface.ts` blends between the road value (`roadConditions`: wet-road speed 0.85, grip 0.8 whenever any snow lies) and the field value (`snowSpeed = 0.06 + 0.34 / (1 + 7·(depth − 4 cm))`, so 40 % of dry road speed in 4 cm, about 10 % in a metre) using `roadSurfaceDistance` over a 2 m verge. The HUD's `snowDescription` literally appends "· roads clear", `roadConditions` is documented as "Cleared roads remain wet/slippery, but never inherit deep off-road snow drag", and the ground shader (`src/client/materials.ts`) masks snow off the road lanes with `roadMask`, with roadside banks drawn as detail geometry. PLAYING.md states "Roads shed snow and stay visible".
- Roads are a flat list of segments: `townRoads(w)` returns `Road { a, b, width }` (232 segments, about 1.7 km, widths 8 and 6 m in the default village; `w.roads` and `retiredRoads` hold creator and expansion additions). `roadDistance` finds the nearest segment, which is what both the physics and the shader use.
- Equipment is inventory: shovel, pickaxe and chainsaw are plain items in `data/items.json` that unlock gathering; trailers (`src/shared/trailers.ts`) are the only vehicle attachments, with a hitch slot per tractor. Tyre tracks already know when a print lands on a road in snow (`scene.ts` near `roadSurfaceDistance(…) < 1`).
- Public money: the Odd Jobs Office pays by `grant()` (parish-minted, logged as `labour task`); towns have a `treasury` (`payTown` in `src/shared/civics.ts`). NPCs choose income in `src/server/npc/routines.ts`/`economy-choices.ts` (jobs, hauling loops, gathering, labour as a fallback) and their text briefings in `strategy.ts` list the ways to earn.

### Snow on roads

- Add **per-segment snow** to the roads: `w.roadSnow: Record<segmentKey, { depth: number; ploughedAt: number }>` keyed by a stable hash of each segment's endpoints (creator roads already have IDs via `retiredRoads`; procedural ones get `${a.x},${a.z}-${b.x},${b.z}`). `advanceClimate` keeps the world depth for fields and, in the same loop, adds `snowfallPerDay × dt` to every segment's `depth` while `precipitation === 'snow'` and melts it with the same solar term, so an unploughed road carries exactly the field depth and a ploughed one restarts from zero. Only segments with depth > 0 are stored; a clear-weather pass deletes zero entries so the record stays small. Offline catch-up uses the same code, so snow falls on roads while the server is idle.
- Road speed follows the road's own snow. `drivingSurface` looks up the nearest segment's `depth` and feeds it through the existing `snowSpeed` curve (speed 0.85 on a ploughed wet road, 0.40 in 4 cm, 0.10 in a metre) and the matching grip reduction, so an unploughed road is exactly as slow as the field beside it — no worse, no better. Walking uses that same surface factor, including a road's own depth once roads keep snow (see Walking speed follows snow depth). The HUD road readout replaces "roads clear" with "Roads: 6 cm snow" / "Roads: ploughed" and the speedometer surface line names the cause.
- Rendering: `roadTexture` writes each segment into the mask with its snow fraction in a second channel, so the shader shows snow on unploughed lanes (tinted and lifted like the field, with tyre ruts where tracks exist) and bare wet asphalt on ploughed ones, with a soft edge at the plough line; the roadside bank geometry grows where a lane has been ploughed (the pushed snow goes to the verge). Segment textures update when a plough pass arrives in the snapshot; the map shows unploughed roads in white and ploughed ones in grey, with a "snowed in" legend in winter.

### The plough blade

- A **plough blade** is a purchasable tractor attachment, not an inventory item: `p.attachments[slot] = 'plough'` bought at any garage for about **24s** (between a shovel at 40d and the cheapest trailer at 36s, priced so a few winters of storms pay it back), fitted to one owned tractor, shown as a front-mounted angled blade on that tractor's model (`vehicle-custom`/`tractor.ts` front mount point; the biplane, boats and walking cannot carry it). It can be removed and refitted at a garage. The Mechanic's yard and Three-bay garage interiors (see the interiors section) show one on a stand when stocked. Optional rear **salt spreader** later; out of scope here.
- Ploughing is passive while driving: with a blade fitted and `raised === false` (toggle with a key / HUD button, default lowered), every movement tick that keeps the tractor within half a road width of a segment with depth > 1 cm clears a strip: the segment's depth drops by the fraction of its area the pass covered (blade width 3.2 m across a 6 or 8 m road means two or three passes to clear fully; store `depth` as the mean and let one pass take `3.2/width` of it). The tractor travels at the _ploughed_ speed while the blade is down and sheds 8 % more fuel per metre; ploughing from a tractor is only effective at or below **20 mph** (the blade caps the tractor's speed at that while lowered; the Swift Kestrel's 40 mph and the Puddle tractor's 29 mph are not available with the blade down), and raising it restores the normal limit. A raised blade does nothing and costs nothing. Deep snow (> 40 cm) ploughs at half rate per pass so a blizzard takes several passes.
- **Pay**: the parish pays by the metre, credited every 50 m cleared: `plough_rate × metres × depth_factor` where `depth_factor` scales from 1 at 4 cm to 2 at 40 cm, so the first pass through deep snow pays most and tidying a nearly clear road pays little. Default rate **0.25d per metre at 4 cm** (the 1.7 km village network pays about 4s 30d per full clearance, which with two or three passes is roughly twenty minutes of driving — well above Odd Jobs labour, in line with a good hauling loop, and paying the blade back in about six clearances; the northern island roads multiply that). Calibrate against `tests/economy-balance.test.ts` before fixing the number. Money comes from the road's town treasury when it has funds (`payTown` in reverse, logged `snow ploughing`), falling back to a parish `grant` like labour so clearing is always paid; the town council can set its own rate and a daily cap in the civic panel. Pay is shown live on the HUD ("Ploughed 180 m · 3d 60") and in the statement; `kudos` rises per 500 m.
- Pay is only for public roads (`townRoads` segments, not farm tracks, the airfield or spaceport pavement, which keep their existing snow behaviour and are cleared by their operators), and only once per fill: a segment pays again only after more snow has fallen on it, so driving back and forth on a clean road earns nothing.

### Snowplough trucks: manufactured, faster, and salting as they go

- A **snowplough truck** is a new vehicle slot (the first truck in the game; same `VehicleDef` schema as tractors with `mode` road, a 3.6 m reversible front blade and a dump-style body) that is **not** sold at the garage. It is built only by the **automotive factory** from the River crossings section (a large, late-game industry consuming metal, gravel and wood), using the same order → production → delivery flow planned there for highway tractor units: a factory owner takes a deposit, the recipe consumes about 120 metal, 40 tools and 20 fuel over a long production run with staffing, and the finished truck is delivered to the buyer's garage (or appears as a parked vehicle outside it once parked vehicles exist). Councils may order one for the parish from the treasury and assign it to a public-works driver, so a town can guarantee ploughing without depending on a private buyer. Target price in the region of **200–300s**, calculated against the factory's input costs and margin rather than fixed in advance.
- **Performance**: the truck ploughs effectively at up to **45 mph** (speed cap while the blade is down; 55 mph with it raised), carries 220 cargo weight when not salting, uses a larger tank and about twice a tractor's fuel per metre, needs the `driver` licence in licence worlds, and cannot tow trailers or enter arena combat. Its blade clears `3.6/width` of a segment per pass, so one pass clears a 6 m lane almost entirely and two clear an 8 m road, and it ploughs deep snow (> 40 cm) at the normal rate rather than the tractor's half rate. Model: a two-axle cab-over with amber beacons (flashing while the blade is down, visible at night through the existing vehicle-lights code), a hinged dump body, a rear spinner, and the blade's snow-roll particle effect from the tractor plough enlarged.
- **Road salt** is a new item (`salt`, weight 1, produced at the mine as a by-product recipe and bought at the quarry or the garage; also importable in worlds without a mine). The truck's dump body is a hopper: the driver loads salt at a stockroom or from inventory (up to 200 units), and while the blade is down and the spinner is on the truck spends **1 salt per 25 m** and marks each segment it clears with `saltedUntil = w.time + salt_duration` (default **2 game days**, scaled down by the current snowfall rate so a blizzard overwhelms it faster). On a salted segment, new snowfall accumulates at **25 %** of the normal rate and the melt term runs even under cloud, so the road stays passable through a normal storm and only needs re-ploughing in a heavy one; `drivingSurface` treats a salted segment with depth under 2 cm as a wet road. A truck with an empty hopper still ploughs at full speed but does not salt and earns only the plough rate. Tractors cannot carry or spread salt (no spreader attachment in this scope). The map shows salted segments with a faint blue tint while `saltedUntil` is in the future.
- **Pay**: a truck earns the plough rate plus a **salting rate** per metre whenever it actually spread salt on that pass (default **0.15d per metre** on top of 0.25d, so about 60 % more than a tractor for the same distance), credited in the same 50 m increments and subject to the same once-per-fill rule — but salting a _clean_ road ahead of a forecast storm also pays the salting rate once per `salt_duration`, so pre-treating before a blizzard is a legitimate job. Salt cost at default prices should leave a clear margin over the salting rate, checked in the balance test. The town rate and cap settings cover both rates; the statement itemises "ploughed" and "salted" metres.

### NPCs

- Resident **haulage businesses or councils** that can afford a truck order one from an automotive factory once one exists in the world and winters have shown unploughed roads costing them (the planner compares last winter's lost hauling hours with the truck's price); until then they use tractor blades. A truck-owning resident runs the ploughing loop at truck speed, buys salt before storms, salts arterial roads first (segments with the most traffic in the last day, which the server already samples for tyre prints), and pre-treats when `weatherEvent` forecasts snow within a day.
- Residents who own a tractor can buy a blade when a snow event is forecast (`weatherEvent` gives `start`/`end` and strength, so the planner sees the storm coming) if their cash exceeds the price plus their household reserve, and the heuristic chooser (`economy-choices.ts`) adds a **ploughing loop**: while any segment within their town has depth > 4 cm and the hourly pay estimate (rate × unploughed metres nearby ÷ time) beats their other options, drive the unploughed segments nearest-first with the blade down, refuel as needed, raise the blade and return when nothing is left. The loop interrupts for meals and ends when pay per minute falls below hauling. Briefings in `strategy.ts` and the resident knowledge base gain "snow ploughing: buy a blade at the garage; the parish pays by the metre" so LLM-driven residents know the path, and the council's road-maintenance budget appears in civic observations.
- A town with no plough owner stays snowed in: that is the point. The public Puddlewick migration gives the parish garage one spare blade in stock and assigns one blade to a long-standing NPC haulier so the first winter after upgrade is not impassable before anyone buys in.

### Per-world rules and migration

- Editor → Rules gains **Road snow**: _self-clearing_ (today's behaviour, default for arena, racing and compact worlds) or _ploughed by players_ (default for Puddlewick after a one-time versioned migration), the plough and salting rates per metre, the deep-snow multiplier, the blade price, the tractor and truck ploughing speed caps, salt duration and the salted accumulation fraction, the truck recipe and whether trucks are available at all (worlds without an automotive factory can allow the council to import one), whether pay comes from the treasury or the parish, and whether NPCs may buy blades or order trucks. Creator-forced snow/snowstorm weather obeys the same per-segment model.
- Update PLAYING.md ("Roads shed snow"), the driving-surface comments, `snowDescription`, the Odd Jobs briefing and ECONOMY.md's income list.

### Tests and acceptance

- Segment depth tracks world snowfall and melts identically when unploughed; a plough pass reduces depth by the covered fraction and resets `ploughedAt`; speed on an unploughed road equals the field speed at the same depth and returns to the wet-road value once cleared; raised blade and non-public pavement earn nothing; the tractor blade caps speed at 20 mph and the truck at 45 mph with the blade down; a truck pass clears `3.6/width` and spends 1 salt per 25 m only with salt aboard; a salted segment accumulates at 25 % and melts under cloud until `saltedUntil`, then behaves normally; salting pays the extra rate only when salt was spread, and pre-treating a clean road pays once per duration; a truck cannot be bought at a garage and is delivered by the factory flow; pay per 50 m with the depth factor and the once-per-fill rule; treasury-then-grant funding and the town rate/cap; NPC chooser buys a blade before a forecast storm and runs the loop until pay drops; migration flips Puddlewick once and leaves self-clearing worlds alone; offline catch-up snows roads in; browser test shows snow on an unploughed lane, bare asphalt behind a plough pass, and the map colouring.

## Planned — Springs, pump-houses and desalination plants

Drinking water should come from two different kinds of place: a **desalination plant** on the ocean shore, and a **pump-house** on fresh water — a natural spring or a river bank. Springs are a new natural feature, rarer in the lowlands and commoner in the hills, with one at the head of every river.

### What exists today

- There is one water producer, `waterworks` ("Shoreline waterworks", 1,200d + 8 wood, 8 blocks, 2 steel, 6 gravel, tier 0; recipe 1 fuel → 12 water per ten-minute batch; sells water at 13.25d, buys fuel at 74d; five models: Intake house, Split pump house, Pump and valve house, Pump tower, Twin pump halls). Water itself is a 5d item that every settler drinks.
- Siting is **ocean-only**: `waterworksSite` in `src/shared/shoreline.ts` demands a dry nine-point foundation above `highWater` and an intake 6–10 m behind the building below `lowWater` − 0.3 m, tested at both tidal extremes; the build action, the construction preview, `reports.ts` and `workplace.ts` all call it, and production stops ("dry intake") if the terrain is edited so it fails. PLAYING.md notes that "inland edited ponds count" because the test is purely a height test against sea level, not a test for _salt_ water, so a creator pond at the right height passes by accident while a northern river (whose surface is well above sea level) never can.
- `nearestWaterworksSite` grid-scans only the original ±224 m village square for NPC founding, so NPCs can never found water on the northern island.
- Rivers are `IslandRiver { id, name, points: RiverPoint[] }` in `w.northernIsland` (five of them, generated by `islandRivers` in `src/server/island-rivers.ts` from a start ring 1,800 m out from the mountain centre and surveyed downhill to the sea); `points[0]` is the head of each. Ponds are `IslandPond` ellipses. `inlandWaterAt(w, x, z)` returns level, depth, flow and bank distance for any river or pond, and `riverDockSite` already does a "dry landing, wet berth" test against it — the pattern a river pump-house needs. Gathering grounds are `ResourceNode { id, item, x, z, capacity, regrowth }` with stable IDs frozen in the island survey, and the map hides them below 50 % zoom.

### Springs as a natural feature

- Add **springs** to the world survey as a new kind of natural site, `w.springs: Spring[]` (`{ id, x, z, flow }`), generated deterministically from the island seed alongside the resource survey and frozen the same way so IDs and positions never move after the first save. Density follows elevation: sample candidate points on the island's terrain, accept with probability rising from about 2 % at 20 m above sea level to 40 % above 300 m, reject anything under 15 m, within 40 m of water, on a cliff face (`cliffNormal.y` test as in the shader) or on a slope steeper than the building limit, and thin them with a minimum spacing of 250 m so the whole island ends up with roughly **25–35** springs, most of them on the mountain flanks and the eastern and north-western uplands and only a handful in the southern lowlands near Puddlewick (the village's own water keeps coming from the shore). Add a **headwater spring** at `points[0]` of every river (five, with the river's `id` recorded on the spring) regardless of the density roll.
- Rendering: a spring is a small wet hollow — a 3 m mossy stone basin with a trickle of water (a short animated ribbon using the river surface material) running a few metres downhill before soaking away, darker damp ground in a 6 m patch, reeds and a few boulders — drawn by the resource-scenery path (`src/client/resource-scenery.ts`, with the natural-not-worked look from the gathering-grounds section) and streamed like other scenery. The headwater springs sit at the top of the existing channel so the river visibly starts there. The map gets a spring symbol (a droplet ring) that follows the gathering-ground zoom rules, and the field guide names them.
- Springs are also a free **drink**: standing within 3 m on foot, a new `drinkSpring` action beside the inventory `use` action offers "Drink from the spring" (quenches like one water, 20-second cooldown, no item), so hikers in the north are not forced to carry water, but nothing can be bottled without a pump-house.

### Two water buildings

- **Rename** the existing kind: `waterworks` keeps its id (saves, NPC chains, recipes and `tradeSymbols` all key on it) but its display name becomes **Desalination plant**, its models are retitled (Intake house → Desalination house, and so on), its description says it draws sea water, and its siting rule becomes the current `waterworksSite` _plus_ a salt test: the intake must be in ocean (`inlandWaterAt` undefined at the intake and below sea level), so an edited inland pond no longer qualifies for a new plant. Existing plants are grandfathered: the resurvey in `reports.ts`/`workplace.ts` keeps using the height-only test for buildings that already exist. Its recipe stays fuel-hungry (1 fuel → 12 water) because desalination is energy-intensive; consider lifting the water price a little in the balance test.
- **New kind `pumpHouse`** ("Pump-house", tier 0, about **900d** and 6 wood, 6 blocks, 1 steel, 4 gravel — cheaper than the plant), with a lighter recipe (**1 fuel → 20 water**, or 0 fuel → 8 water when a world turns on gravity-fed springs) and the same wage, staffing and stockroom behaviour. Siting (`pumpHouseSite` in a new `src/shared/fresh-water.ts`, mirroring `waterworksSite` and `riverDockSite`): dry foundation, then **either** a spring within 8 m of the building's intake point (the building snaps so its intake sits on the spring; one pump-house per spring, enforced like berth reservation) **or** an intake 6–10 m behind the building that lands in a river or pond with at least 0.4 m depth by `inlandWaterAt`, with the landing above the river's local `level` + 0.3 m (floods would otherwise reach the floor). Ocean shoreline is refused with "Pump-houses need fresh water; build a desalination plant on the coast". River pump-houses do not reserve the reach, so docks and fords are unaffected, but two pump-houses must be 60 m apart on the same river to stop a bank being lined with them. Five architectural models in the `works` family (Spring house, Well house and pump room, Riverside pump station, Stone spring chamber, Pump tower and header tank), a map icon distinct from the plant's, and a catalogue entry in `data/buildings.json` and the website gallery.
- Headwater pump-houses draw the most reliable water: the headwater spring's `flow` is highest, and a world rule can make output scale with `flow` (0.6×–1.2×) so hunting for a good spring matters.

### NPCs, migration and rules

- `nearestWaterworksSite` becomes `nearestWaterSite(w, p, kind)` that scans the actual world extent (the island survey's bounding box, sampled on a coarse grid then refined) for both kinds; the chain planner in `src/server/npc/chains.ts` chooses a pump-house when a free spring or river bank is nearer or cheaper to run than the coast, and the perception "thirsty → waterworks/market/starport" list includes `pumpHouse`. Briefings and the knowledge base describe both buildings.
- Migration (one-time, versioned): rename nothing in saved data; retitle the existing `waterworks` in the catalogue; generate the spring survey for public Puddlewick and freeze it alongside the resource survey; no existing building moves or loses production. Private and heightmap worlds get springs from their own seed the first time they load after the upgrade, or none if the world rule is off.
- Per-world rules in Editor → Rules: **Springs** on/off, target spring count and the elevation curve, whether every river gets a headwater spring, spring drinking on/off, pump-house recipe (fuel per batch and output), whether output scales with flow, the river spacing rule, and whether desalination requires true ocean (off keeps today's height-only test for creator worlds that use edited ponds as "sea"). Creator mode can also place or delete springs by hand like resource nodes.
- Docs: PLAYING.md's water section (the "Shoreline waterworks" paragraph and the "inland edited ponds count" note), ECONOMY.md, NORTHERN_ISLAND.md (springs and headwaters), the building catalogue and the field guide.

### Tests and acceptance

- Spring survey is deterministic per seed, respects elevation weighting, spacing, water/cliff/slope exclusions and the headwater guarantee; springs freeze with the survey across restarts and portable export. Desalination refuses an inland pond for a new site and still runs an existing pond-sited plant; a pump-house is accepted on a spring (snapping to it) and on a river bank with depth ≥ 0.4 m, refused on the ocean shore, on a taken spring and within 60 m of another river pump-house; river pump-houses do not block docks or fords. Recipes and prices pass `tests/economy-balance.test.ts`; NPC founding picks the nearer of the two kinds and can found on the northern island; drinking at a spring quenches with cooldown; preview images for both kinds and the spring scenery render in `scripts/building-architecture-preview.ts` and `scripts/northern-island-preview.ts`.

## Planned — Open bay doors and modelled interiors

Workshops, hangars and sheds should look like places where work happens: during business hours the big doors stand open and you can see a furnished interior through them, with the doors rolling or sliding shut at closing time and in the night.

### What exists today

- Every building is a hollow shell. `src/client/buildings.ts` and `src/client/building-architecture.ts` build the exterior from `box`/`wallFinish`/`roof` volumes; there is no floor, no back wall seen from inside and no interior prop anywhere. The bay door is a single two-triangle `PlaneGeometry` textured with `finishMaterial(look.bay)` (frame, planks, bracing and shutter ribs are baked into the texture) placed flush on the front face. `loadingBayXs` in `src/shared/building-facade.ts` gives one bay per industrial volume, three for a garage wider than 12 m; the `shed` branch in `buildings.ts` gives two bays to garage, warehouse, factory, shipyard and hay barn. The Puddlewick hangar (`b.aircraftHangar`) has a fixed 14.5 × 5.5 m dark opening with two white 3.5 m leaves at its edges, a lintel, rails and the "PUDDLEWICK • FLYING CLUB" sign; the starport cargo hangar (`plan.volumes[2]`, port family) has two 10 × 9.5 m roller-shutter planes. All of them are static.
- `industrialKinds` (`src/shared/building-facade.ts`) already lists the 25 kinds that get bay doors: farm, feedmill, sawmill, quarry, mason, mine, rareMine, forge, brewery, workshop, refinery, factory, shipyard, warehouse, composter, brickworks, concreteWorks, carpenter, garage, boathouse, dairy, sheepfold, piggery, haybarn, plus `port`-family starport models.
- The only animated building parts are the spaceport fence gate (`animateGate` in `src/client/spaceport-complex.ts`: `openness` damped toward a target when a player is near, rotating `pivot.rotation.z`) and lighthouse rotors; windows glow at night through `window-light` emissive maps keyed by `userData.lightSource`. There is no "business hours" concept for buildings: production is gated by staffing (`ownerOperating`, `jobVacancy` in `src/shared/staffing.ts`, `activeWork` rule), not by the clock, and the day is 600 real seconds (`DAY_SECONDS`).

### Doors that open and close

- **Door state** is derived, not stored: a pure `doorOpenness(w, b)` in a new `src/shared/building-doors.ts` returns 0–1 from the time of day and the building's state so every client and the server agree without extra sync. Default rule: open from 07:00 to 19:00 game time when the building is not under construction and is either staffed (`ownerOperating` or at least one employee or NPC with `job === b.id`) or is a public service building (hangar, garage, town workshop); closed at night, during construction and when the business has no operator. A building whose owner or worker is currently inside (see the walking section below) is open regardless of the hour. Give each building a small deterministic offset (hash of `b.id`, ±20 minutes) so a street of workshops does not open in unison.
- **Geometry**: replace the baked bay texture with a real opening. Cut the aperture from the front wall, build a door leaf group behind the lintel in a `userData.bayDoor` pivot, and choose a motion per kind: **sectional roll-up** (garage, warehouse, factory, feedmill, concrete works, starport) — six hinged panels that translate up a track and curl back along the ceiling; **sliding leaves** (hangar, sawmill, boathouse, haybarn, shipyard) — two leaves that slide outward along the existing rails; **hinged barn doors** (farm, dairy, sheepfold, piggery, forge, carpenter, workshop, brewery, brickworks) — two leaves swinging outward about 110°. Keep each leaf a single mesh with the current finish texture so the closed state looks like today.
- **Animation**: the scene's per-frame loop (`GameScene` render tick, where `animateGate` already runs) damps each door's `openness` toward `doorOpenness(w, b)` over 4–6 s, with a soft start and stop; roll-ups rumble and sliders squeal through `sound` only when the player is within 40 m. Doors within 120 m animate every frame; further ones snap to the target state when their chunk streams in. Doors never block movement — the player's collision uses `buildingBlocksMovement`, which stays as is — but a closed door hides the interior so nothing inside is drawn.
- **Low-quality mode**: on `aclone.quality = low` the doors still open and close (it is one pivot per door) but the interior detail set is reduced to the floor, back wall and the two or three largest props.

### Interiors

- **Shell**: each kind with a bay door gets a floor slab (concrete, planks or flagstones by kind), inner wall faces using the inverse of the exterior finish, a ceiling with rafters or trusses, a back wall, and two or three interior lamps (`lightSource` keyed to the building so they glow at night and in the dusk before closing). Interiors are built once per building like the exterior and attached under the building root with `frustumCulled = true`, and the whole interior group is set `visible = openness > 0.02` so closed buildings cost nothing. Props are merged into as few meshes as possible per material (the same `batch` approach as `spaceport-complex.ts`) with a budget of about 6,000 triangles per standard interior and 15,000 for the hangar and starport.
- **Puddlewick hangar (`aircraftHangar`)**: a biplane parked nose-out just inside the door, a second with its cowling off on trestles and its engine on a stand beside it, a propeller hung on the back wall, two red roll-cab tool chests, an oil drum with a hand pump, a workbench with a vice, a wall of hanging spanners, fuel cans in a bunded tray, a tyre rack, a wind-sock pole visible through the rear window, and a pilot's board with chalk weather. - **Garage** (Motor shed, Three-bay workshop, Service station, High clearance repair shop, Mechanic's yard): one or two two-post lifts, one raised with a tractor on it (the existing tractor models, a different slot per garage by `hash(b.id)`), a pit with a grate on the Mechanic's yard model, a wheeled tool chest per bay, an air compressor with a coiled yellow hose on a reel, a bench grinder and parts washer on a long bench, a tyre-changer and balancer, a rack of oil cans and filters, hanging work lights, a pegboard of hand tools and a calendar; in the three-bay model the third bay is the parts store with shelving and a counter.

## Planned — Tractors stay where you leave them; walking, doors and arrival animations

Vehicles should be physical objects in the world rather than a mode of the player. If you walk away from your tractor, it stays where it is; if you sign off, your empty tractor is still parked there; if you go indoors, it waits outside. Getting in and out, and entering and leaving buildings, are visible movements rather than instant state changes.

### What exists today

- A player _is_ their vehicle: `p.vehicle` is a slot number and `switchVehicle` in `src/shared/tractors.ts` swaps it in place, parking fuel in `p.parkedFuel[slot]` and remembering `p.lastTractor`. "Walk on foot" (`vehicle` action, slot 5) and "Return to tractor" (`returnTractor(p)`) are both instant and position-free: the tractor dematerialises when you walk and reappears under you wherever you are when you return. Only the position of _your current body_ exists.
- Home: the `home` action sets `p.atHome = true` and the client hides your mesh (`mesh.visible = !p.atHome` in `src/client/scene.ts`), so a tractor driven to a home vanishes while you are indoors and is under you again on `outside`.
- Sign-off: `leave()` in `src/server/app.ts` sets `online = false` and the snapshot filter (`q.online || id === me.id`) stops sending the player to other clients, so the tractor disappears with the person. Offline players still exist in `w.players` with `x`, `z`, `vehicle` and `parkedFuel` intact, which is what the new behaviour needs.
- The only objects that already behave as "left in the world" are trailers (`w.trailers`, each with `x`, `z`, `heading`, `attached`, rendered by `TrailerScene` and solid in `move`) and moored boats (`w.mooredBoats`, rendered and reserved at berths, left at the landing when a boat owner disembarks at a pier or river dock). Those are the pattern to generalise.
- Interaction: pressing `e`/Ctrl or clicking a building calls `openInteraction(b.id)` and the panel opens immediately (`src/client/main.ts`), from any range the building panel allows (18 m for most actions via `actionBlocker`). Humans exist as a model (`createHuman` in `src/client/human.ts`, `animateHuman` gait in `human-gait.ts`), used for the walking vehicle slot and NPC figures; there is no mount/dismount or door animation.

### Parked vehicles as world objects

- Add `w.parkedVehicles: Record<id, { owner, slot, x, z, heading, y, fuel, condition, paint, parkedAt, inside?: buildingId }>`, keyed by `${owner}:${slot}`. **Walking away** (`vehicle` → slot 5 from a motor vehicle) creates the record at the player's current position and heading, moves the slot's fuel into it (replacing `parkedFuel[slot]`), and leaves the player standing beside the driver's door (1.4 m to the left of the vehicle's axis). **Returning** requires the player to be within 3 m of the parked record (`'Walk to your tractor first'` otherwise, with a map pin and the distance shown on the "Return to tractor" button through `actionBlocker`), deletes the record and restores fuel. The tractor-fleet "recall outside" flow (`tractor-shop.ts`, the garage's "Owned tractors can be recalled outside") becomes **drive out of the garage**: choosing a different owned tractor at a garage parks the current one in the garage's `inside` slot (it appears in the garage interior from the section above) and you leave in the other.
- **Running out of fuel** therefore means what it says: walk to a fuel seller (shop, garage, refinery, another player's tanker), buy fuel into inventory, walk back, and refuel the parked vehicle from inventory while standing beside it (a `refuelParked` action, reusing the fuel-item-to-tank rate of the existing `refuel` action and the "help refuel" flow in `src/shared/player-aid.ts`, which already lets another player fill your tank). Show "Out of fuel — your tractor is 340 m north" on the HUD while you walk.
- **Home**: `home` parks the vehicle you arrived in at the nearest free kerb point outside the house (the building's door-side face, offset to avoid the path and other parked vehicles), so the player's body is hidden but a tractor stands outside. `outside` puts you at the door on foot; the "Depart in tractor" and "Depart on foot" buttons in the home panel let you choose. Lodging rooms work the same way at the hotel/B&B.
- **Sign-off**: `leave()` no longer removes the vehicle from view. If the player is driving, the vehicle is parked at their position and the person is removed; if walking, only the person goes. Snapshots include `parkedVehicles` for everyone (they are small) and the client renders them with the existing tractor/boat/biplane models, no driver figure, lights off, engine off, name tag "Hank's Swift Kestrel". On sign-in the player appears in the driver's seat if their record says they were driving, otherwise on foot where they stood. `maxOfflineDays` absence handling (`src/shared/simulation.ts` near `lastSeen`) also removes the player's parked vehicles.
- **Solidity and tidiness**: parked vehicles are added to `move()`'s obstacle set like trailers (sliding stop, no damage) and to the `buildingBlocksMovement`-style apron and construction-site checks so nobody builds over a parked tractor. To stop towns filling with abandoned machines, a world rule sets a **parking grace** (default 7 game days, which at `DAY_SECONDS = 600` is 70 real minutes of server uptime; measured on the world clock so offline catch-up counts) after which a vehicle left on a public road is towed to the owner's home kerb or the nearest garage for a fee (`garage` stock gains the vehicle in `inside`), with a notice in the owner's statement. Vehicles parked on their owner's own property, in a garage or at a hangar are never towed.
- **Boats, aircraft and trailers**: boats already moor; a biplane left on the apron becomes a parked vehicle at the apron point (and blocks the apron, which the existing 'apron occupied' check should recognise). Hitched trailers stay hitched to a parked tractor. The lifeboat, charter and trawler crew flows (`fishingVoyage.members`, `hitch`) are unchanged.
- **NPCs** use the same rules: their routines already walk between jobs, so an NPC that leaves its tractor must come back for it; the planner treats "my tractor is at X" as a hard constraint and may choose to walk short trips.

### Animations

- **Mount and dismount**: a 1.6 s client-side sequence when `vehicle` changes between a motor slot and walking: the human figure appears at the driver's door, steps up (cab vehicles) or swings a leg over (open tractors), the seat figure fades in; reverse for dismount. During the sequence inputs are ignored and the camera holds. Other players see the same sequence because they receive the `vehicle`/`parkedVehicles` change and run the same animation from the parked record's door position.
- **Entering a building**: `e`/Ctrl or click on a building first checks `actionBlocker` range (so the walk is at most 18 m); then the client sends `approach` (server records `p.task = { kind: 'entering', building, end }` so the player is immobile and `online` others see it), dismounts if driving (vehicle parks at the kerb point), and plays a scripted walk from the current position to the building's door using `buildingFacade`'s entry point (`entries[]` in `building-architecture.ts`, or the bay door for industrial kinds), opens the door (hinged doors swing 90° via a `userData.doorPivot` added to the `door()` helper; bay doors are already open in business hours), steps through, and the door closes. Only when the task completes does `openInteraction` show the panel; on Escape or on leaving the panel, the reverse plays and the player stands outside the door on foot. Hidden-player state while inside reuses `atHome`-style `inside: buildingId` so the figure is not drawn. The whole sequence is 2–4 s depending on distance; a world rule **Arrival animations** (full / quick 1 s / off) lets fast-paced or low-end worlds skip it, and the first-steps hints explain the walk. Mobile tap-to-interact and controller `Interact` use the same path.
- **Home**: the same approach walk with the house door, then the person is inside; leaving plays the door and a short walk to the kerb, then (if "Depart in tractor") the mount.
- **Businesses with bay doors** use the pedestrian side door where the model has one, otherwise the open bay; closed buildings outside business hours still let you in through the side door, and the interior lamps come on while someone is inside.

### Migration, rules and acceptance

- One-time migration: for every existing player, if `vehicle` is a motor slot, nothing changes (they are driving); `parkedFuel` entries without a record become parked vehicles at the player's home kerb (or the parish garage for the homeless), so no fuel is lost and no machine appears in a river. The legacy single-tank inference in `switchVehicle` is kept for old saves.
- Per-world: **Vehicles persist in the world** (on — default for Puddlewick; off keeps today's instant switching for arena, racing and compact worlds), parking grace and towing fee, whether sign-off parks or hides the vehicle, arrival animation mode, and whether NPCs are bound by parking. All in Editor → Rules with migration flags like `boatDeathRulesVersion`.

## Planned — Forestry and logging

Replace the static "logs" gathering grounds with real trees that are felled, bucked, hauled, milled and replanted, so timber becomes a livelihood with visible consequences on the landscape. All numbers below are starting targets and every rate, limit and permission is a per-world rule.

### What exists today

- Logs come only from `logs` gathering grounds (`src/shared/resources.ts`): a node holds 18 logs, regrows one every 30 game minutes, needs a carried chainsaw, and yields 3 logs per 20 s (6 per 12 s with the `forester` skill). The sawmill recipe turns 2 logs into 4 wood with the `lumberjack` skill; wood feeds the workshop, carpenter and winery. The Forester log wagon carries 900 weight (a log weighs 3). Two northern provinces are log-rich and two log-poor.
- Trees themselves are client-side decoration generated per chunk by `woodland(w, x, z)` cover and a seeded scatter (`src/client/countryside.ts`); the server does not know any individual tree. The solid scenery item above moves tree placement into a shared deterministic generator with stable per-tree IDs (chunk plus index), which this work depends on.

### Tree model

- Every procedural tree gets a species (pine, birch, broadleaf — the three the scatter already draws), a deterministic natural age from its seed, and a **growth stage** derived from age: sapling (first half game year), pole (to 1.5 years), mature (to 4 years), old growth. A game year is 365 days of 600 s (about 61 real hours), so a felled stand needs roughly a real week to be worth cutting again; tune per species (pine fastest, broadleaf slowest, broadleaf highest yield).
- State is a sparse overlay, `w.forestry`: felled trees keyed by tree ID with the time and feller (a stump for a few game days, then natural regeneration from seed unless the ground was built on, padded or kept clear), planted trees with species, planting time and planter, and a per-player and per-town felling ledger. The overlay prunes entries once a tree has fully regrown to its procedural state so the save does not grow without bound. Rendering scales the shared tree instance by stage and swaps in a stump; the map shades forest density from the generator plus the overlay.
- Yield by stage and species: nothing from a sapling, 2–3 logs from a pole, 6–10 from a mature tree, 10–14 from old growth; the forester skill raises the yield and halves the task time as it does today.

### Felling, bucking and hauling

1. **Fell.** Stand within reach of a trunk with a chainsaw (or a cheap new **axe**, three times slower and limited to pole-stage trees) and start a felling task of 15–45 s scaled by girth. The server picks the fall direction away from the feller, checks the fall line for buildings, people and vehicles and refuses if occupied; the client plays the cut, lean and crash with the existing contact-shadow and snow systems updated. No damage to anything in this slice.
2. **Buck.** The felled trunk lies on the ground as a world object. A second task saws it into logs that appear as a **log pile** at the stump, persisted and visible to others; piles belong to the feller for a grace period, then become free for anyone to take, which stops abandoned piles blocking a clearing forever.
3. **Load and haul.** Logs are taken from a pile into inventory (3 weight each, so a walker carries few) or into a Forester log wagon standing within a few metres, by hand at a slow rate or with the backhoe's loader (earthmoving item) much faster. Haul to a sawmill bid, a shop, a construction site or the boat and tanker flows as any other good.
4. **Clear for building.** With solid trees, `constructionSiteReason` and earthworks surveys refuse a footprint containing standing trees with "Fell 4 trees first"; the site plan marks them. Village shade trees and town-centre trees are protected.

### Ownership, permissions and sustainability

- Natural forest is a commons with rules: a per-player felling allowance per game day inside a town boundary (a town rule, like road permission; unlimited outside towns by default but configurable), protected zones, and visible depletion so a clear-cut hillside stays bare for a year. Towns may charge a stumpage fee into the treasury per tree felled within their boundary.
- Planted trees belong to the planter until mature; saplings are a new good produced by farms with the `horticulturist` skill (a nursery recipe), planted with a short task on clear dry ground, and watered like crops only in their first season. A **woodlot** is an optional claimed forest parcel attached to a sawmill or home (reusing the pasture-parcel claim and fence code) in which only the owner may fell and in which replanting is tracked; it gives a forestry business a managed asset.
- NPC residents can take forestry as an earning plan: fell within allowance, haul to the sawmill bid, and replant when they own a woodlot; the briefing reports allowance and stumpage.

### Migration and economy

- Keep the existing `logs` grounds through the transition so NPC plans and task IDs survive; once trees are fellable in a world, reduce the grounds to "windfall" sites with a fraction of today's capacity, then retire them in a later release. Retune the sawmill supply and log price with the new flow, and keep the log-rich provinces meaningful by making their stands older and denser.
- Per-world rules: growth rates per species, yields, task times, felling allowance and stumpage, grace period for piles, woodlot size and price, protected zones, and whether forestry is enabled at all (compact worlds keep their hand-placed trees and today's grounds).

### Tests and acceptance

- Deterministic tree IDs and stages across server/client and restarts; felling refusal on an occupied fall line; yield by stage, species and skill; pile ownership grace; log wagon and hand loading rates; construction refusal and clearing; allowance and stumpage per town; sapling production, planting, growth and woodlot ownership; regeneration and overlay pruning; NPC forestry plan; map density; performance of the overlay with thousands of felled trees in `scripts/island-performance.ts`; browser coverage of felling, bucking and loading animations.

## Planned — Piloted spaceflight

Space is a route planner. The aim is to make it a place you fly: a ship with mass, a tank, a fitting, and a sky that has stars, planets and other ships in it. You undock from the parish spaceport, cross a system, jump to another star, and bring the ship down onto a world under that world's gravity. Trade, courier work and survey stay; they happen in the cockpit instead of on a card.

### What exists today

`data/galaxy.json` has seven systems (Hearth, Brindle, Farthing, Loam, Lantern, Rime, The Vessel) on a flat parsec map, each with a hazard of 0–4, and six ships (Modest shuttle, Cargo apology, Needle courier, Longview explorer, Breakwater escort, Unidentified teapot). `jumpQuote` in `src/shared/galaxy.ts` prices a hop at the ship's credits-per-parsec plus a shielding surcharge of 2cr per hazard, reduced by the hull's shield fraction. The hop takes `6 + 2 × fuel-cost` seconds. You cannot trade or steer during it. The shipyard panel says there is no random cargo loss and no space combat. Fittings are three fleet-wide levels of drive and three of hold (+2 pc and +20 cargo each). Galactic credits and a small cargo ledger are separate from parish cash and inventory; the spaceport exchanges them at `exchangeRate`, capped per real day. Courier jobs, a 15cr survey, and a free Hearth rescue for a broke empty ship are already there.

The spaceport itself is a graded pad, a fence, a gate and a terminal on the parish (`spaceportSite`, `spaceport-complex.ts`). A scenery rocket flies a scripted loop (`spaceportFlight`): ignition, an 800 m rise, time away, a landing. Nobody pilots it. Taking off is a menu. Walking into deep water sends you to the parish spawn; there is no vacuum and no orbit.

### Three ways of flying

One ship, three regimes. Weapons, the cargo hatch and the landing gear work only in the first.

- **Local flight.** Vacuum around a station, a star or a world. Six axes: forward and back, slide left and right, rise and fall, and pitch, yaw and roll. Flight assist is on by default: the ship holds the attitude you leave it in and cancels drift when you centre the stick, so it flies more like the boats than like a tumbling brick. Assist off is true coasting, for people who want it. Boost spends the engine reserve and is not free speed. Top speed depends on the thrusters and on how the power is shared (below). There is no air and no stall.
- **System cruise.** Faster-than-light travel inside one star system. The drive compresses space ahead of the ship. You cannot stop. Proximity to a star, a planet or a station caps your speed: pointing at a mass slows you, pointing away lets you run, and the cap only gets large far from everything. Too close to a body, or too fast for the distance you have left, and the drive dumps you into local flight. That emergency drop heats the ship and damages the hull. Hardpoints, the hatch and the gear must be stowed or cruise will not light. A targeted station or world shows the speed and the alignment that will drop you cleanly; flying that bracket is the skill. Turn rate is best near half throttle.
- **The jump.** A charge, interruptible by damage or by shutting the drive down, then a crossing to another system. You arrive in cruise, close to that system's star and aimed at it. The first thing you do is turn, or the star's exclusion zone drops you on top of it. Range is no longer a hard wall on the route planner. A drive has an optimal mass and a comfortable reach; past that, fuel and heat climb sharply, and past the absolute reach the jump will not light.

Distances on the first chart stay the seven systems. A neighbouring hop, flown properly, is a journey of a few minutes: a charge of tens of seconds, a cruise measured in minutes, and an approach of about a minute. A world rule can stretch the chart later. The manoeuvres are the point; a delivery should not become an hour.

### Gravity and coming down onto a world

Every body has a surface gravity, in multiples of a standard g, and an exclusion radius. In local flight near a body the ship is pulled: thrust, inertia and that acceleration, integrated on the server in the same spirit as `fly`. Cruise will not stay lit inside the exclusion radius.

The approach is its own regime, between cruise and the pad:

- Drop from cruise only inside a speed band for your altitude. Faster than the band is an emergency drop.
- A glide wants a descent angle of roughly **5° to 60°**. Steeper, or a sink the glide cannot hold, dumps you into local flight while you are still high, under gravity, with whatever speed you kept.
- The altimeter shows orbital height, the drop height, the sink, and the body's gravity. Above about **1 g**, the safe glide is the shallow end of that band, and the landing burn has to be started earlier. A botched high-g touchdown damages the hull and can destroy the ship. Below 1 g the same burn sends you back up; the assist will not hover for you.
- Gear down, a flat enough patch, and a sink inside the limit settles the ship. The parish spaceport pad is the first legal patch, and Hearth's world is about **1 g**. Other bodies in a system can be airless, heavy, or only a station in orbit.
- The parish terrain, roads and buildings load for the last part of the glide, not for the whole system. Cruise and local flight above the world are a sky scene. The island stream stays on the ground.

Later, a ship can set down away from the pad, and a small ground buggy can be carried in an optional slot. That waits until landing on the pad is reliable. On foot under a landed ship uses the existing walker; vacuum worlds need a suit module or the hatch will not open.

### Fuel

Fuel is hydrogen in a tank, measured in tonnes, not a line of credits.

- Undocked, the power plant burns a little all the time. Cruise burns faster than local flight. The jump does not sip: at the moment the charge completes it takes a lump from the main tank. That lump grows with the ship's mass over the drive's optimal mass, and faster than linearly with distance, so two short jumps spend less than one long jump over the same gap.
- A small reserve feeds the modules. When it empties it refills from the tank. The jump ignores the reserve and takes the lump from the tank itself. The gauge shows tonnes left and the current burn per hour.
- Docked, the burn stops. Stations sell fuel for credits. A fuel scoop, an optional module, lets you skim a main-sequence star in cruise by holding the scoop band: too shallow and you collect nothing, too deep and the exclusion zone or the heat kills you. Not every star is scoopable.
- Empty in space, the ship is stranded. Another player can transfer fuel. The existing free Hearth rescue remains for a poor, empty, uncontracted ship; anyone else pays a tow. Life support is a core module: with the canopy broken or the module unpowered, a timer starts, and running it out destroys the ship.

Credits stop standing in for fuel. They pay for fuel, repairs, fittings and fines. The old per-parsec credit price and the hazard surcharge retire once the tank exists. A jump in progress on the old timer is allowed to finish.

### Ships

The six named hulls become real ships, with a size, a mass, a hardpoint layout and a set of internal slots. They keep their roles:

- **Modest shuttle** — small, cheap, the ship you get back when you cannot afford a rebuy. Short reach, little cargo, few hardpoints.
- **Needle courier** — small and fast, a thin hold, the ship you choose when the cargo is a parcel and the clock matters.
- **Cargo apology** — medium trader. A large hold, a slow turn, a drive that is cheap on fuel and poor in a fight.
- **Longview explorer** — medium. A long reach, a scoop-friendly build, a small hold, room for a scanner.
- **Breakwater escort** — medium. Armour and shield space, a modest hold, the ship that can stay when the others run.
- **Unidentified teapot** — the late, expensive hull. The longest reach, the largest hold, fast and light on fuel, with strong shielding.

Size matters at the pad. Small and medium ships use the spaceport pad that exists today. A later heavy freighter, slow and huge, needs a larger pad and will not fit a small station. Switching ships at a shipyard stays free when the hold and any contract fit, as it does now.

### Fittings

A ship is a list of modules, changed at a station shipyard, stored per ship. The three fleet-wide drive and hold levels go away; what you bolted on is what you fly.

Eight cores are required, each with a size that has to fit the slot and a grade from E up to A:

- Bulkheads, the power plant, thrusters, the jump drive, life support, the power distributor, sensors, and the fuel tank.

Optional slots take a shield generator, extra cargo racks, a fuel scoop, hull reinforcement, a scanner, repair or refuel gear, and (later) a buggy hangar. Hardpoints take weapons and tools, in sizes from small to huge. Utility mounts take shield boosters, chaff, heat sinks and point defence.

How a weapon is mounted matters. A fixed mount hits harder and aims where the nose aims. A gimbal tracks inside a cone and needs the sensors. A turret tracks more loosely and hits for less. Thermal weapons eat shields; kinetic weapons eat hull. Ammunition is finite and bought at the station; energy weapons draw the weapon reserve instead.

The plant has a budget. Deploying hardpoints adds draw. Each module has a priority from 1 to 5; when the plant cannot feed everything, the highest numbers shut off first. A sensible fighting ship keeps thrusters, life support, sensors and the guns above the scoop and the cargo hatch.

The distributor has three reserves — shields, engines, weapons — and six shares, at most four in any one. Shares set how fast that reserve refills. Shields also resist more while they have share and reserve left: four shares roughly doubles how long a given shield lasts under fire, two shares adds about half again. Engines with share turn and boost sooner. Weapons with share fire longer before the reserve is empty. Moving the shares during a fight is part of flying the ship.

Heat comes from the plant, from firing, and from charging the drive. Past 100% the ship starts to cook; well past that, modules fail. A heat sink dumps it. Running cold makes you harder to see on sensors. Silent running, vents closed, does that on purpose and cooks you faster.

### Combat, law and losing the ship

Combat happens in local flight, with the hardpoints out. Cruise is for running and for being pulled out of cruise.

- Shields fall first, then hull. A target can be a whole ship or one module: the plant, the thrusters, the drive. Breaking the plant finishes a ship; breaking the drive stops it leaving; breaking the thrusters stops it turning.
- In cruise, a hostile can try to drag you into local flight. You and they fly a short contest of alignment. Winning it keeps you in cruise. Losing it drops both of you, hardpoints hot, with some damage to the loser. Pirates want cargo. Security wants warrants.
- Safe systems (hazard 0, which is Hearth and its quiet neighbours) have security ships and treat an unprovoked attack as a crime. Hazard systems spawn pirates and answer more slowly. Attacking a clean ship, firing on a station or blocking a pad builds a fine or a bounty, visible to other players and to security. Paying the fine at a station in that jurisdiction clears it. A bounty is claimed by whoever destroys the ship, security included.
- Destroyed, you pay a rebuy: a world-rule fraction of the hull and the fitted modules, starting at **5%**, taken in credits. Pay it and the ship comes back as fitted, at the last station. Refuse it, or be unable to pay, and you are back in a stock Modest shuttle at Hearth. The parish character is untouched: cash, land, tractors and the body on the island stay. The ship ledger is the thing at risk.
- Player-versus-player weapons follow the world fighting switch. With fighting off, players cannot harm each other; NPC pirates in hazard systems can still be enabled on their own. With fighting on, the safe-zone rules that already protect towns apply to the spaceport pad and to a docked ship. The flight-combat entry covers aircraft guns. This entry covers ships. They do not share a damage table.

### The station and the parish

Undocking and docking are flown. You request a pad, get the existing spaceport pad, and have a limited time to land on it. Sitting in the approach without clearance is a fine, then a shot from the station. An optional docking computer, a module, flies the last alignment for you and can be destroyed or switched off.

On the pad you are back in the parish, on foot, under the rules you already know. The exchange, the courier board, the survey desk and the shipyard are services of this station and of stations in the other six systems. Those other stations are real rooms in space first: a pad, a shop, a board. They do not need a second island on day one. Linking a station to another hosted world stays the galaxy-passport path in `GALAXIES.md`; credits, ships and space cargo still do not cross servers.

Courier packages occupy hold space and are delivered by docking at the destination, not by waiting out a timer. The three space goods keep station prices from `stationPrice`, and hauling them is flying the route. A survey is a scanner pass in cruise, sold at a station, replacing the flat 15cr button once scanners exist. The scenery rocket can stay as an unpiloted freighter on its own schedule.

### Per-world

Jump reach and fuel curves, cruise speed caps, approach angles, body gravities, fuel price, rebuy fraction, whether NPC pirates exist and from which hazard upward, whether players can shoot each other, heat limits, scoop availability, and whether piloted flight is on at all. The default is on for the public parish: you can fly the shuttle, cruise Hearth, jump the seven systems, and land on the pad, with pirates only from hazard 2 up and with player weapons off unless fighting is on. ### Order

### Tests

Assist holds attitude and kills drift; assist off conserves a coast. Cruise speed falls as you point at a mass and rises as you point away; an exclusion-zone crossing and an overspeed drop both damage the ship and leave you in local flight; a drop inside the published speed band does not. Jump fuel for two short hops is less than one hop of the same length, and a ship over optimal mass pays more. The scoop fills only inside the band and overheats outside it. An empty tank strands the ship, a transfer refuels it, and the free rescue still fires for a poor empty shuttle. A 1 g approach on the pad settles inside the sink limit; a steep glide and a high-g landing without a burn destroy the ship. The island is not streamed during cruise. Fittings that exceed the plant shut off by priority when the hardpoints deploy. Shields fall to thermal damage and hull to kinetic; a rebuy restores the fit, and a refused rebuy returns a stock shuttle at Hearth without touching parish cash or land. An interdiction contest has a winner. With fighting off, a player shot does nothing to another player. Courier cargo moves only when the destination pad is docked. A saved cruise, a saved glide and a saved half-charged jump resume after a restart. Browser coverage for undock, cruise, jump, approach and dock on the real pad.
